## One incident leaves traces in three places

In [lesson 9](/en/devops/slo/) we saw the burn rate rule page once, four minutes after the bad deploy. This lesson follows the same incident on, from the deploy to the post-incident report, gathering its traces from three places that we already have.

**The first place** is Git. A deploy and a rollback are dated tags. In the exercise we build a repository with the tags `v1.0` (three days earlier), `v1.1` at 14:00 on day 12, and `v1.1.1` at 14:19, which is a commit that does `git revert` on the commit of v1.1. Reading them back with `git for-each-ref --sort=creatordate` gives what we set (ran).

```text
v1.0   2026-01-09T14:00:00Z
v1.1   2026-01-12T14:00:00Z
v1.1.1 2026-01-12T14:19:00Z
```

**The second place** is the measurements: the burn rate rule from [lesson 9](/en/devops/slo/), which paged at minute 4 after the deploy. **The third place** is what people do, which no tool records by itself. In the exercise we assume that the person on call takes 5 minutes from the bell to pick it up and open the dashboard, and another 10 minutes to decide and start the rollback. These two numbers were set by the author, not measured on a real team. Putting all of it in order gives a list of events (ran).

| Time | Event | Comes from |
|---|---|---|
| 14:00 | Version 1.1 is deployed | tag v1.1 |
| 14:04 | The burn rate rule pages | measurements |
| 14:09 | The person on call picks it up | assumption (5 minutes) |
| 14:19 | Tag v1.1.1 and the pipeline deploys | tag v1.1.1 (assumption of 10 minutes) |
| 14:21 | The error rate is back to normal | measurements |

## Three numbers to measure an incident

The table gives three times. Time to detect is the time from the deploy to the bell, 4 minutes. Time to acknowledge is from the bell to somebody picking up, 5 minutes. Time to mitigate (or recover) is from the deploy until the error rate is normal, 21 minutes (ran).

Let's compare three ways of working on the same bug. Choose one in the lab and watch the orange band, which is the time the requests still fail, and the bar below it, which says how much of the month's budget the incident eats.

<IncidentLab client:visible lang="en" />

The results of the three ways in the exercise (ran). The first has no alert rule, so we wait for a customer to write in at minute 35 and then roll back for another 10 minutes. It is back to normal at minute 45 and eats 34% of the month's budget. The second has the bell and a rollback by hand, back to normal at minute 21, eating 16%. The third has the bell and a one-command rollback (which is the `git revert` through the pipeline that we saw in [lesson 7](/en/devops/gitops/)), taking 3 minutes after pick-up, back to normal at minute 12, eating 9%. The same bug, but the damage differs by nearly a factor of four. The numbers point to two things that make the difference: how soon we know about the problem, and how short the way back is.

</Step>

## A postmortem that does not hunt for the guilty

When the incident is over the team should sit and look at it. The DevOps Handbook calls this a blameless post-mortem, a way to build a just culture. The reason the book gives is that if we punish the engineer who made the mistake, nobody wants to tell the details we need to understand how the failure happened, which guarantees it will happen again. But if the engineer is not punished, they are willing, even keen, to help the whole company avoid the same mistake. That is what creates the learning of the organisation (book: The DevOps Handbook chapter 19).

In the exercise we generate the report from the list of events above with code. It has the sections Summary, Impact, Timeline, What went well, What went wrong and Action items, and we check that no person's name is in it (ran).

```markdown
# Postmortem: failed requests after release 1.1

## Summary
Release 1.1 (`add coupon search`) made about 20 percent of requests fail
from 14:00 until the rollback was live at 14:21.

## Impact
16% of the 30 day error budget of an SLO of 99.9 percent.

## Timeline (UTC)
- 14:00 deploy: version 1.1 is released (tag v1.1)
- 14:04 page: the burn rate alert fires
...
## Action items
- Add a test for requests with a coupon (owner: the team that owns search).
- Release search changes to 10 percent of the traffic first (lesson 5).
```

Notice two things. **First**, the number in Impact comes from the real data of the incident (16%), not from a feeling. **Second**, there is no name anywhere (the exercise checks that the commit author does not appear in the report). The action items are owned by a team, not a culprit, and point back at the gaps in the system: no test covers requests that use a coupon, and the release went to everything at once, which is what [lesson 5](/en/devops/release/) proposes to fix (a canary or a flag).

<Callout type="note" title="What was not tried">
The exercise builds a postmortem from data, but did not try a real meeting, on-call duty, swapping shifts, or an incident tool such as PagerDuty. The author did not check that the sections match any particular template. The DevOps Handbook does not give a section-by-section template to compare with.
</Callout>
