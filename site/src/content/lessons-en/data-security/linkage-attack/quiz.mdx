## Check yourself

<Question
  client:visible
  lang="en"
  q="An attacker does not have our table, but knows that the neighbour Eve was born on 9 August 1987, lives in zip 10330 and is male, and the table we released has all these columns. What can he do?"
  options={['Nothing, because the table has no names', 'Find the rows that match on zip, birth date and sex. If there is one, he knows the diagnosis and salary of Eve', 'He can only guess the sex']}
  answer={1}
  why="This is a linkage attack in its simplest form: use the identifiers we already know to point at the one row that is left. The exercise measures that with exact values and a voter list the attacker names 1,409 of 2,000 people correctly."
/>

</Step>

<Step kind="question">

<Question
  client:visible
  lang="en"
  q="When a join finds exactly one voter, is the row certainly that person's?"
  options={['Certainly, always', 'Not certainly. The owner of the row may not be on the list and just happen to share the values with another voter. The exercise finds 1 such row of 1,410, and it happens more as the data gets coarser', 'Never']}
  answer={1}
  why="The attacker knows only that no other voter fits the row. He does not know whether the owner of the row is on the list. With birth year and a 5 digit zip he names 711 rows and 502 are right, which is about 209 wrong."
/>

</Step>

<Step kind="question">

<Question
  client:visible
  lang="en"
  q="Why does cutting the zip to 2 digits while keeping the full birth date help so little?"
  options={['Because zip is not a quasi identifier', 'Because the full birth date and the sex already tell people apart almost completely, so zip adds the least', 'Because a short zip is easy to guess']}
  answer={1}
  why="In this table the birth date has 1,900 values among 2,000 people and tells nearly all of them apart by itself. The exercise result is that a 2 digit zip with the full birth date still names 1,363 correctly. The birth date has to be coarsened first before anything is seen."
/>

</Step>
