## One number can leak

Mr. A's team wants to publish the number of customers with the diagnosis HIV in our table, which counts 351 out of 2,000. One number, no rows at all (run: `exercises/05_dp_basics.py`). It looks as safe as anything could be.

Picture an attacker who knows the diagnosis of everybody in the table except Ann, and wants to know whether Ann has it. If he counts 350 among the others and the published number is 351, he knows that Ann does. One true number is enough when the attacker already knows nearly everything. We call two tables that differ by exactly one person **neighbouring tables**.

The way out, **differential privacy**, is to make the answers of two neighbouring tables impossible to tell apart, by adding random noise before the release, so that an attacker who sees 351 does not know whether it came from a true 351 or from a 350 that got noise to make it 351 (book: Hands-On Differential Privacy chapter 2 pages 26 to 29, and Practical Data Privacy chapter 2 page 30).

</Step>

## Laplace noise, and a definition that can be checked

The answer released is the true value plus a random number from the **Laplace** distribution whose width (scale) is the sensitivity divided by epsilon.

```python
def laplace(rnd, scale):
    u = rnd.random() - 0.5
    return -scale * math.copysign(1, u) * math.log(1 - 2 * abs(u))

answer = 351 + laplace(rnd, 1 / epsilon)       # a count has sensitivity 1
```

Sensitivity is the most that the answer can change when one person is added or removed. For a count it is 1, because one person changes the count by at most 1 (book: Practical Data Privacy chapter 2 page 43). Epsilon is a number we choose: the smaller it is, the more private. The function makes the random number from a uniform number with a transform formula.

The definition says that a mechanism is **epsilon-DP** if, for every pair of neighbouring tables and for every possible answer, the probability of that answer from one table differs from the other by at most e to the power epsilon (book: Hands-On Differential Privacy chapter 2 pages 29 and 38). We do not rest on the book alone. The exercise works out the ratio of the densities of the two tables, 351 and 350, at every point from 330.0 to 369.9, which covers below, between and above the two true values. The result is that the worst ratio equals e to the power epsilon exactly: 1.105 at epsilon 0.1, 1.649 at 0.5, 2.718 at 1 and 7.389 at 2. The figure is not only never exceeded, it is reached at the points outside the stretch between the two values.

Look at the picture in the lab below. Choose an epsilon and press play. Noisy answers from two worlds, one with Ann in the table and one without her, fall onto the same number line and pile up, the world with Ann above the line and the world without her below. A detective sees only the number and guesses which world it came from, and his score is counted live. The smaller the epsilon, the more the two piles sit on top of each other, and the closer his score gets to a coin flip.

## The price is error

The overlap of the two curves measures how hard it is to tell the two worlds apart. The exercise works it out by adding up the smaller of the two densities at each point. At epsilon 1 they overlap by 60.7% (which is e to the power minus 0.5), at 0.1 by 95%, and at 2 only 36.8% is left. An attacker who sees one answer and has to guess which world it came from can hardly guess at epsilon 0.1.

But the noise that protects Ann also makes the number drift. The average error of an answer equals the scale exactly. The exercise measures it over 50,000 answers per epsilon: 99.8 at epsilon 0.01, 10.0 at 0.1, 2.0 at 0.5, 1.0 at 1 and 0.2 at 5. Ten times more privacy (epsilon divided by ten) means ten times more error. For the count of 351 people, the error is only 0.3% at epsilon 1 but 28% at epsilon 0.01.

</Step>

## A mean needs a clip, and small groups hurt more

A count has sensitivity 1 every time, but a mean salary does not. If values are not limited, one person with a salary of 10 million moves the mean of 2,000 people by about 5,000, which the exercise works out. The sensitivity then has no bound. The fix is to **clip** the values into a range set in advance (in the exercise 20,000 to 80,000), and then the sensitivity of the mean of n people is (80,000 minus 20,000) divided by n.

What is asserted: for 2,000 people the sensitivity is 30, for 200 people 300, and for 20 people 3,000. A group of 20 people therefore gets noise 100 times larger than a group of 2,000. Against a mean of about 45,000 that is 6.7% against 0.07%. This is why differential privacy protects large groups well, while small groups, which are the most at risk, get the most noise.

</Step>

<Step>

## Randomized response: each person adds the noise

Everything so far assumed that somebody we trust holds the whole true table and adds noise before the release. If we trust nobody, each person adds noise before sending their answer. The simplest way is called **randomized response**. In the exercise each person flips a coin. If it is heads (probability 0.75) they answer truthfully. If tails, they flip again and answer with the result (book: Hands-On Differential Privacy chapter 2 page 30).

A "yes" therefore comes from a person who really has it with probability 0.875, and from one who does not with 0.125. The ratio is 7, which makes epsilon ln 7, or 1.946. The collector who sees one person's "yes" does not know whether it is true. But adding up the answers of 2,000 people and subtracting the coin gives an estimate of the share, 0.1687 against the true 0.1755, which the exercise checks is within 3 percentage points.

The price is accuracy. At the same epsilon, adding noise centrally gives an error under 1 person, while having each person add their own gives about 26, roughly 50 times more (the square root of 2,000 as the table grows). That is the price of not trusting the collector. The book Practical Data Privacy discusses collecting data this way through the examples of Apple and Chrome (book: Practical Data Privacy chapter 3 pages 74 to 77), which we did not run.
