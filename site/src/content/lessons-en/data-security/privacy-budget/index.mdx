## The same question again and again

In the last lesson we published the count of 351 patients with noise at epsilon 0.1, where one answer is off by about 10 people. Now suppose our system lets anyone ask, and every time someone asks it draws fresh noise. The attacker asks the same question over and over and averages the answers.

Independent noise has an average of zero. When we average many answers it cancels. The exercise tries it (run: `exercises/06_privacy_budget.py`). One answer is off by 13.1. The average of 10 answers is off by 3.49, the average of 100 by 0.26, and the average of 2,000 answers is 351.01, only 0.01 from the truth. The attacker knows the true count almost exactly, although every answer was "protected" by epsilon 0.1.

The exercise measures the rate in another set. Over 400 repetitions one answer is off by 9.45 on average, while the average of 100 answers is off by 1.15, about 8 times more exact (in theory 10 times, a little less here because we take the average of the absolute error). That is the square root of the number of answers.

And the price the attacker really paid is that 1,000 answers at epsilon 0.1 add up to epsilon 100, which is a very weak guarantee (from the last lesson, the part where the two worlds overlap is e to the power minus epsilon/2, which at epsilon 1 is still 60.7%, but at epsilon 100 is almost gone). The guarantee of differential privacy has to count every answer together, not each answer alone (book: Hands-On Differential Privacy chapter 2 page 43).

</Step>

## An accountant

The first defence is a keeper of the budget who adds up the epsilon of every answer already given, and refuses when the total would be exceeded. This is called **sequential composition** (book: Hands-On Differential Privacy chapter 2 page 43 and Practical Data Privacy chapter 2 page 45). The exercise writes an accountant that takes a budget of 1.0 and tries queries of 0.1 each.

```python
acct = Budget(total=1.0, seed=3)
for _ in range(20):
    acct.count(is_hiv, 0.1)        # answers 10, and the 11th is refused
```

The result is exactly 10 answers, then `privacy budget exhausted`, and the ledger adds up to the budget allowed. The counter must always be kept on the side that holds the data. If the askers are different people, they still have to be counted together, or two people working together are the same as one asking twice.

The other way is not to draw fresh noise for an old question. The exercise stores the answer to "total HIV count" and asks again 1,000 times. The same piece of answer comes back every time, and the budget spent stays at 0.5. The average of 1,000 answers is that answer itself, no closer to the truth.

</Step>

## Splitting the budget over many queries

If we have a total budget of epsilon 1 and must answer k queries, each gets epsilon 1/k, which means the noise scale is k. What is asserted: with 1 query the average error is 1, with 10 queries about 10 per answer, and with 100 queries about 100 per answer, which against 351 patients is large (28%) and for smaller groups useless.

With a limited budget we must choose which queries matter most. One query answered accurately is worth more than a hundred answered uselessly. Splitting unevenly, for example giving the important query more, was not run by the author.

</Step>

## A histogram is paid once: parallel composition

There is a good exception. If the queries are counts over groups that **do not overlap**, for example the number of people in each of 12 zip areas, a person lives in only one zip (the exercise checks that the 12 groups add up to 2,000). Removing one person therefore changes only one of the twelve numbers, and by only 1. So we put noise of scale 1 divided by epsilon on all 12 cells and pay only epsilon once, not 12 times. If we wrongly think we must split the budget over 12 cells, the noise grows 12 times for nothing. The exercise checks the ratio of densities of the whole histogram against a neighbouring table, and it does not exceed e to the power epsilon.

This is called **parallel composition**. It applies only when the groups really do not overlap. If one person can be in several groups, such as counting by diagnosis when one person can have several illnesses, we are back to paying the combined price.

## Rounding after noise costs no budget, but it biases

Once we have a number with noise we want to tidy it, for example round it to a whole number, or turn a negative into 0. This is **postprocessing**, and it costs no extra budget, because it is a function of the answer already released and does not touch the real data again (book: Hands-On Differential Privacy chapter 2 page 44). The exercise checks that rounding gives whole numbers.

But there is another price, bias. Suppose a group whose true count is 0 and epsilon is 0.5. The raw answers average 0, but if negative values are changed to 0 the average of the answers becomes 1.0 (the scale divided by two). The exercise measures 0.994 over 100,000 answers. An empty group therefore always looks as if it holds about one person. An analyst who adds up hundreds of small groups rounded this way gets a total that is too high.

<Callout type="note" title="A limit of this lesson">

Our accountant adds epsilon directly, which is the simplest and loosest way to combine. There are tighter ways of combining that spend less budget when very many queries are asked (advanced composition). The author did not run them, and did not try the accountant of a real library.

</Callout>
