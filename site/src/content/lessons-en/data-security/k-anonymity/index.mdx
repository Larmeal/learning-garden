## If every group has at least five people

From the last two lessons we know the problem is people who are alone in their group. If we group people by their quasi identifier values (here zip, age band and sex) and demand that every group holds at least k people, an attacker who knows those values for somebody is left with at least k rows that fit, and cannot tell which is the target's. A table like that is called **k-anonymous** (book: Practical Data Privacy chapter 2 page 54).

There are two ways. **Generalize** means to widen a value, for example the birth year into a ten year band, or a 5 digit zip into 3 digits. **Suppress** means to remove the rows of groups that are still too small, whole groups at a time. Both cost something: the first loses detail, the second loses rows.

The exercise works out every combination of (age band 1, 5, 10, 20 years) and (zip 5, 4, 3, 2 digits) on the 2,000 synthetic people, and measures three things: k (the smallest group), the number of rows to suppress for k to reach 5, and the average group size (run: `exercises/04_k_anonymity.py`).

</Step>

## Coarsen, and look at the price

Choose an age band and a number of zip digits in the lab below. The red bar is the rows that must be thrown away. The bar of average group size says how coarse the data is: the bigger the groups, the less detail is left.

What the exercise asserts: with the exact birth year and a 5 digit zip, k is 1 and 1,872 of 2,000 rows must be suppressed, because they are in groups smaller than 5. With 5 year bands and a 3 digit zip, k is 3 and 38 rows must go. With 10 year bands and a 3 digit zip only 10 rows (0.5%) must be suppressed, and the remaining 1,990 rows are in groups of five or more.

In this table a 2 digit zip is the whole city (every zip starts with 10), so 10 year bands and a 2 digit zip give only 12 groups, each of 82 or more people, and no rows are suppressed, but very little detail is left. The average group grows from 1.9 people to 166.7. A 20 year band adds nothing over 10 years, because the people were born over only 55 years.

## Everybody in the group has the same illness

A k-anonymous table says nothing about the sensitive column. k only counts the people in a group and does not look at how many diagnoses are in it. Take the release with 10 year bands and a 3 digit zip, where every group already has at least 5 people.

In our synthetic data zip 10900 is the area of a specialist clinic. The author set it so that most people there have the same diagnosis. The exercise counts that 169 of 181 people in that area have the diagnosis HIV. After grouping, 5 groups with 62 people in all have a single diagnosis. One group is zip 109, born in the 1970s, female, with 10 people, all of them with HIV.

An attacker who knows only that his target lives in that area, was born in that decade and is female learns the diagnosis without knowing which row is the target's. That is a **homogeneity attack**. The table passes k-anonymity fully, and it still leaks. The book speaks about this limit under "why isn't k-anonymity enough" (book: Practical Data Privacy chapter 10 page 267).

A common fix is to require that every group holds at least l different diagnoses (l-diversity). The exercise checks only that 2-diversity fails for this release, and that even the groups of area 109 that are not all HIV still have at least 80% of it. Fixing it means merging groups or suppressing more, which costs more rows. Other levels of l-diversity were not run.

</Step>

## How big must k be

The author did not run a search for a suitable k. The 5 in this lesson is an example chosen to make the picture easy to see. In real work the k used for health data is often higher, and it depends on what the attacker already knows. The book Building an Anonymization Pipeline places this in the Five Safes framework, which we come back to in the final lesson (book: Building an Anonymization Pipeline chapter 3 page 44).

Another limit of this method is that if an attacker holds several versions of our table, one coarsened one way and another coarsened another way, he can put them together and narrow the groups. The author did not try this.

[The next lesson](/en/data-security/differential-privacy/) changes the way of thinking completely. Instead of making the table coarse enough, we do not release rows at all. We release only the answers to questions, with noise mixed in, and there is a mathematical definition that can be proven for how safe that is.

</Step>
