## The right hand is a clock

Players who learn by ear often remember a strum as a sound ("dum dum dum-dum") and copy it. That works until you must play a pattern you have never heard, or play slower than before and the pattern falls apart. The sound you remember does not tell your hand what to do during the silent parts.

The rule that works for every pattern is this: **the right hand swings down and up all the time and never stops.** Go down on the main beats (1 2 3 4). Go up on the off-beats ("&"). A silent part of a pattern means the hand swings past the strings without touching them (a ghost strum). Almost every pattern that sounds complicated is just a choice of which beats of this steady swing let the pick touch the strings.

There are three benefits. The timing holds by itself, you never have to think about when to go down or up, and when you change the speed the pattern is still correct.

</Step>

## Count the subdivisions

One bar in 4/4 has four beats. Each beat can be divided into smaller parts, and these divisions are the language we use to write strumming patterns.

| Division | How to count | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| Quarter notes | 1 2 3 4 | Slow ballads |
| Eighth notes (8th) | 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & | Most pop, rock and folk |
| Sixteenth notes (16th) | 1 e & a 2 e & a ... | Funk, fast pop |
| Triplets | 1 la li 2 la li ... | Swing, blues, shuffle |

A style is called **straight** if the two "&" parts split the beat equally. It is called **swing / shuffle** if the first part is longer than the second (for example two thirds of a triplet). If you are not sure which one a song uses, count "1 & 2 &" along with it. If it fits, the song is straight. If it feels like walking with a limp, it is a shuffle.

The details of note values and time signatures are in the music theory path, in the lesson "Rhythm and measures".

</Step>

## Muting makes the rhythm sharp

The sounds you do not want matter as much as the sounds you want. A chord that rings on and on makes the rhythm one big lump.

- **Mute with the left hand.** When you relax the fingers that press, keep the fingertips touching the strings but do not press down to the fret. The sound stops at once. Use this to make short notes (staccato) on the beats you choose.
- **Mute with the right hand (palm mute).** Rest the edge of your right palm lightly on the strings next to the bridge. You get a short, dull sound. It suits rock riffs and fast rhythms. Move the palm closer to the bridge for a clearer sound, and move it away for a duller sound.
- **Dead strum with an X.** Mute all the strings with the left hand (touch lightly, do not press to the fret) and strum with the right hand. You get a percussive knock like a drum. Use it in place of the snare on beats 2 and 4, and you can make funk or rock and roll straight away.

<Callout type="tip" title="Try it now">
Play Em at 80 bpm and strum down on every beat. On beats 2 and 4, mute the strings with the left hand so they become a knock. It sounds like a drum at once, and you do not need a drummer.
</Callout>

## A practice order to make the rhythm part of you

1. **One chord, one pattern** at 60 bpm, until the right hand swings by itself without thinking.
2. **Add a second chord and change on beat 1 of the bar.** If you cannot change in time, ghost strum, following the rule from the first step.
3. **Change the pattern in the middle of a song.** For example, use a light down-up in the verse and full bars in the chorus. This trains you to control the volume (dynamics).
4. **Play with the real thing.** Set a drum loop or play along with a song you know. Play without stopping through the whole song.

**This week's task:** choose the "Basic pop" pattern and play the progression G C D Em (from the chords lesson) at 90 bpm, clean in every bar. Then switch to "Off-beat" and listen to how the mood changes.
