## Your ear is an asset, but it needs a process

Someone who has played by ear for ten years already has the most important skill for working out songs: an ear that can tell sounds apart. What is usually missing is a **process** and a **language to write down what you hear**, so you have to start again every time. This lesson gives you both.

**Tools**: a program or app that slows the audio down without changing the pitch, and that can loop a section you choose. There are free and paid ones. Any one that does these two things is fine. You also need a guitar that is in tune.

</Step>

<Step>

## The order for working out one song

Work from the big picture to the details, not from the first note to the last.

1. **Find the key (tonic).** Play along one note at a time until you find the note that "sounds like going home". It is often the last note of the song or of the final section. Confirm it by checking what the first or last chord is.
2. **Listen to the bass.** Each chord usually has its root as the bass note. Find the bass note bar by bar, and you get the root of each chord.
3. **Decide the chord quality.** On the same root, a bright chord is major and a sad chord is minor. Try both and choose.
4. **Check with numbers.** Convert the chord names to Roman numerals in that key (I IV V vi) to see whether they match a progression you already know (the music theory lesson "[Harmony in a key](/en/music-theory/diatonic-harmony/#changing-key-with-numbers)").
5. **Work out the melody.** Use the scale of the key as a map, and search phrase by phrase.

<Callout type="try" title="Try it now">
Pick one song you already play well. Do steps 1 to 3 and write the result on paper: what is the key, what chords does it have, what is the name of each chord. Players who learn by ear often find that they have always known this song in their hands, but never named what they play.
</Callout>

</Step>

## Turn melodies into numbers

Remembering note names on the neck (C D E) ties a melody to one key. Scale degree numbers (1 2 3 ... of the key) let you change key at once.

| Melody in C | C C G G A A G | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Twinkle Twinkle | C C G G A A G | 1 1 5 5 6 6 5 |
| In the key of G | G G D D E E D | 1 1 5 5 6 6 5 |

When you hear a melody, ask yourself "which degree is the first note, and does the next note go up or down by how many steps?" instead of asking "which note is it?" In time your ear starts to hear the scale degrees (1 sounds like home, 5 sounds like a support, 7 sounds like it wants to go home). This is the root of ear training.

**Practice changing key**: choose a 4-chord loop you can play (I V vi IV). Play a simple melody over it in three different keys every day. This way you can change key at once to suit a singing voice.

</Step>

<Step>

## Learn songs by their structure

A song is not one long line. It is built from sections that repeat. Learning by section takes less time than playing from start to end.

1. **Make a song map.** Write the structure (intro, verse, chorus, bridge, outro) and the chords of each section on one sheet of paper.
2. **See which sections repeat.** A section that repeats is learned only once.
3. **Learn one section at a time.** Start with the chorus, the part you will play the most.
4. **Connect the sections.** Practice the joins between sections separately, because the place where people most often make mistakes is where the section changes.

When you learn songs this way, you often see that different songs use the same set of chords. This is what music theory explains.

</Step>

<Step>

## Record yourself and move on

**Record every week.** Set your phone to record two minutes of the song you are learning. When you listen back, check only three things: is the beat steady, are the notes clean, and how does it sound when you listen as a listener, not as the player. Keep the recordings and compare them once a month. Progress you can hear is the best encouragement.

**Practice notebook.** Write the date, what you practiced, the bpm you played correctly, and what you will do next. It is like the table from the first lesson.

**After this path, your options**

| Next path | Good when | What to learn |
|---|---|---|
| Playing with a band or a drummer | You want to play with other people | Timing, listening to others, simple bass lines |
| Fingerstyle | You like playing alone | Right-hand patterns (p i m a), alternating bass patterns |
| Jazz and extended chords | You want more complex chords | 7, 9, 11, 13 chords, ii-V-I (the music theory lesson "[Beyond the key and chord movement](/en/music-theory/beyond-diatonic/)") |
| Songwriting | You want to write your own | Song structure (the music theory lesson "[Melody and song form](/en/music-theory/melody-form/)") |
| Sound and gear | You want to shape your own tone | Amps, effects, recording |

This path does not yet cover the last three topics (fingerstyle, jazz, sound and gear). They are listed in `subjects/guitar/docs/overview.md` as the next topics.

**Final task**: choose one song you have never played. Work out the chords by listening, without looking at sheet music or chords from the web, using the steps above. Make a song map, then play it with a metronome and record it. That is the measure of whether this path has really changed the way you play.
