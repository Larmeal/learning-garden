## Where you are coming from, and what is missing

Playing for almost ten years without lessons does not mean you are weak. People who learn by ear usually have three good things that are hard to get from books: an ear that notices bad intonation, a feel for groove, and songs that they can really play.

The gaps that appear in this group follow a similar pattern.

| What you usually have | The gap that usually comes with it |
|---|---|
| You can play the chords and riffs you like | Chord changes are slow, or you stop to look, when you meet an unfamiliar chord |
| You can play songs by ear | You do not know which key a song is in or what job each chord does, so you cannot transpose |
| You improvise with one pentatonic box | You do not know the note names on the neck, so you get lost outside the usual box |
| Your right hand plays by feel | Your timing wobbles when you play with a metronome or with other people |
| Technique that works for you | Tension in the wrist or shoulder, and for some people pain with no clear cause |

Answer these five questions honestly. The lesson for each question you answer "no" to is where you should start.

1. Can you point to every note on string 6 and string 5 within one second per note? (lesson "[The fretboard map](/en/guitar/fretboard/#the-first-two-strings-to-learn)")
2. Can you change G, C, D, Em with one chord per bar at 80 bpm without a mistake? (lesson "[Open chords and chord changes](/en/guitar/chords/#changing-chords-the-heart-of-this-lesson)")
3. Can you play a pop strum at 100 bpm without your right hand ever stopping its swing? (lesson "[Rhythm and strumming](/en/guitar/rhythm/#the-right-hand-is-a-clock)")
4. Can you play all five boxes of the minor pentatonic in a key you choose yourself? (lesson "[Scales on the fretboard](/en/guitar/scales/#minor-pentatonic-the-first-scale-to-know-well)")
5. Can you hear a short melody and find its notes on the neck within five minutes? (lesson "[Learning songs by ear and building a repertoire](/en/guitar/ear-songs/#the-order-for-working-out-one-song)")

</Step>

## Playing and practicing are different things

Playing means doing what you can already do, for fun. Practicing means choosing something you cannot do yet and repeating it in a piece small enough to get right. Most guitarists who play every day but do not improve are playing only the old material, because the old material feels good.

These practice rules work for every lesson on this path.

1. **Choose a small piece.** Not "practice scales" but "change from G to C in 4 bars" or "this two-bar lick".
2. **Go slow enough not to make mistakes.** The speed at which you are right every time is your starting speed. If you miss often, you are too fast.
3. **Repeat it correctly.** A rule many people use is three correct repeats in a row before you raise the speed (this is a working rule, not a law of science, so adjust it to yourself).
4. **Add a little at a time.** Raise the metronome by 4 to 5 bpm each time. If you add speed and then make mistakes, go back one step.
5. **Stop when you are tired.** Practicing on with tight hands teaches your hands the wrong movement.

<Callout type="warn" title="Pain is a signal to stop">
Tired fingertips are normal. But if you feel sharp pain in the wrist, forearm or elbow, or numbness in the fingers, stop and check your posture and your grip pressure ([next lesson](/en/guitar/technique/)). If it does not go away within a few days, see a doctor. Do not practice through pain.
</Callout>

## The metronome is a measuring tool

Timing that "feels steady" when you play alone often wobbles when something real measures it. The metronome is not there to force you. It tells you where you are in the beat right now.

Here is an order of use that adds difficulty one step at a time.

| Step | Method | What it trains |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Click on every beat (1 2 3 4) | Playing exactly on the click |
| 2 | Click only on beats 2 and 4 | Holding beats 1 and 3 yourself, like a snare drum |
| 3 | Click only on beat 1 of each bar | Holding the whole bar yourself |
| 4 | Four bars of click, four bars of silence, repeat | Checking that you are still on time during the silence |

Step 2 works very well for people who play by ear, because it makes you the one who creates the missing beats.

You can use a metronome app on your phone, or the player in the lesson "[Rhythm and strumming](/en/guitar/rhythm/)" ([lesson 5](/en/guitar/rhythm/) of this path), which has a click you can turn on and off.

## Tune accurately before every session

A string that is slightly out of tune teaches your ear a wrong sound. So tuning before you practice is part of practicing.

Standard tuning from string 6 to string 1 is E A D G B E. The most accurate way is a tuner, either a clip-on tuner on the headstock or a phone app.

When you have no tuner, use the fret 5 method: compare a fretted note with the open string next to it.

| Pluck | Compare with |
|---|---|
| String 6, fret 5 | Open string 5 (A) |
| String 5, fret 5 | Open string 4 (D) |
| String 4, fret 5 | Open string 3 (G) |
| String 3, fret **4** | Open string 2 (B) |
| String 2, fret 5 | Open string 1 (E) |

String 3 is the exception because G to B is four frets apart (a major third), not five. This affects every chord shape and scale you will learn next (see the numbers in the lesson "[The fretboard map](/en/guitar/fretboard/#shortcut-octaves-and-neighboring-strings)").

<Callout type="tip" title="Check with a harmonic">
Touch a string lightly at fret 12 and pluck it. You get a clear note one octave higher. If this note does not match the note you get by pressing the same string down at fret 12, the bridge is not set well (intonation). A guitar technician can fix this. Tuning alone cannot fix it.
</Callout>

## A 30-minute practice schedule

Doing this every day works better than practicing three hours once a week, because your hands and brain remember better when repetitions are close together.

| Minutes | What to do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm up | Stretch the wrists, play slowly so that your fingers cover every fret |
| 5 | One technique | Alternate picking at 60 bpm, or a chord change that keeps catching you |
| 10 | New material | The lesson you are working on in this path |
| 7 | Play a song | A song where you can use what you just practiced |
| 3 | Write it down | The bpm you got right, and what you will practice tomorrow |

The last line matters most. If you write down the bpm you got right, you see progress as a number, which is better than "I feel like I am improving".
