## One shape, every chord

Open chords have one problem: they use open strings, so you cannot move them up the neck. A barre chord solves this. The index finger takes the job of the nut, and the other fingers rebuild the old open chord shape behind it. With no open strings, the shape can slide to any fret, and the root of the chord moves with it.

Two shapes to know first:

- **E shape** (from the open E chord): the root is on string 6.
- **A shape** (from the open A chord): the root is on string 5.

The chord name comes from the note under the index finger (the barre) on the root string. This is why the lesson "[Fretboard Map](/en/guitar/fretboard/#the-first-two-strings-to-learn)" asks you to memorise string 6 and string 5 first.

## A barre technique that does not tire your hand

Most people who struggle with barre chords squeeze with the thumb and the index finger at the same time. This tires the hand quickly and still does not sound clear. The method that works is to change the position and the angle, not to add force.

1. **Use the side of the index finger, not the pad.** Rotate the index finger a little so the bony side does the pressing. The pad is soft and has creases, and these are where strings often slip out.
2. **Stay close to the fret wire**, as with every note in the lesson "[Posture and Hand Technique](/en/guitar/technique/#left-hand-press-just-enough-not-as-hard-as-you-can)".
3. **Pull toward yourself with the weight of your arm.** Let the left elbow and the weight of the arm help press the strings instead of squeezing with the thumb. The thumb only supports from behind.
4. **Check one string at a time**, the same way as in the chords lesson. A buzzing string is often one that lies across a crease of the finger. A small change of the index finger angle can fix it.
5. **Practice at the fret where the sound is easiest to get on your guitar.** Many people find frets 5 to 8 easier than fret 1, but it depends on the string height (action) of each guitar.

If it still does not work, lower the difficulty step by step: first a half barre (the top three strings), then the full barre.

<Callout type="warn" title="If the sound is still bad after practice, check the guitar">
If the strings are very high above the frets, or fret 1 is harder to press than usual, this may be a problem of the guitar setup (action, string weight). A guitar technician can adjust it. It is not only a problem of your fingers.
</Callout>

## Power chords: only the root and the fifth

A power chord (written 5, for example G5) has only two notes: the root and the fifth above it (sometimes the root is added an octave higher). It has no third, so it is neither major nor minor. It works with both. It suits distortion because fewer notes do not clash with each other when the sound breaks up.

```text
G5 (root on string 6)   C5 (root on string 5)
e|-----------           e|-----------
B|-----------           B|-----------
G|-----------           G|---5-------
D|---5-------           D|---5-------
A|---5-------           A|-3---------
E|-3---------           E|-----------
```

This shape can slide along the whole neck. The chord name is still the note of the index finger on the root string. Use the index finger and the ring finger (some people add the little finger), and mute the other strings with the pad of the index finger.

</Step>

## CAGED: five shapes of one chord

We started with two shapes (E and A). Now look at the whole picture. The five open major chords you know, C A G E D, can all be moved up as barre chords (the way the E shape and the A shape do). For one major chord there are five shapes that follow each other along the whole neck with no gaps.

Choose a chord and look at the shapes one at a time.

<CagedLab client:visible lang="en" />

Practical uses:

- **Change key at once.** If you know that G can be played at fret 3 (E shape), fret 5 (D shape), fret 10 (A shape), and so on, you can choose the position closest to the part you are playing now.
- **Each shape has its own character.** The shapes that do not use the low strings (D and C when moved high) sound thinner and brighter. The shapes that use all six strings (E and G) sound fuller.
- **A frame for scales and arpeggios.** The five pentatonic boxes in the lesson "[Scales on the Fretboard](/en/guitar/scales/#minor-pentatonic-the-first-scale-to-know-well)" sit on top of these five chord shapes.

A word of caution: CAGED is a map, not theory. It tells you where the notes of a chord are on the neck, but it does not tell you why. Most of the answers are in the scales and chords lessons of the music theory path.

</Step>
