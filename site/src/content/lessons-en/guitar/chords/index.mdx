## A chord is three or more notes sounding together

A chord is made of three or more notes. The most basic structure is the triad, which stacks notes two steps apart in a scale (the details are in the music theory path, lesson "Chords: triads and seventh chords"). A guitar has six strings but a triad has only three notes, so the guitar repeats some notes in different octaves. The result is a fuller sound while the chord name stays the same.

**Reading a chord diagram**

- The vertical lines are the strings. The far left is string 6 (low E) and the far right is string 1.
- The horizontal lines are the frets. The thick one at the top is the nut.
- Dots show where to press. The number in a dot is the finger (1 index, 2 middle, 3 ring, 4 little).
- A **○** above a string means an open string. A **×** means do not play that string.

## Check the sound one string at a time

Playing well but not listening closely is where players who learn by ear often get stuck. A chord that you can play in a song may hide a dead string that you do not notice.

**The one-by-one test**

1. Place your fingers in the full chord shape.
2. Pluck the strings one at a time from string 6 to string 1 and listen to whether each one rings clearly.
3. If a string sounds muffled or buzzes, fix that string, not the whole chord.

Common causes: the pad of a fretting finger touches the next string (fix it by curving the finger joint more), or a finger is not pressing close to the fret wire (lesson "[Posture and hand technique](/en/guitar/technique/)"). Do the one-by-one test regularly until your ear catches the faults by itself while strumming.

</Step>

## Changing chords: the heart of this lesson

How fast you change chords decides whether you can play a song in time, more than the speed of your right hand. There are four principles.

1. **Move the whole shape.** Lift all fingers at the same time and put them down at the same time. Do not place them one by one, because the hand remembers the shape as one block.
2. **Find the fingers that do not need to move (anchor fingers).** Two chords often have a finger that can stay where it is. Examples:
   - **Am to C**: the index finger stays on string 2, fret 1, and the middle finger stays on string 4, fret 2 in both chords. Only the ring finger moves, from string 3 to string 5, fret 3.
   - **Em to Am**: the middle and ring finger shape slides down one string (strings 5 and 4 to strings 4 and 3), then you add the index finger on string 2.
3. **Look and think ahead.** Before the bar ends, your eyes and mind are already on the next chord. Your fingers start to float toward the position.
4. **Accept silence, but not slowness.** While practicing, if you cannot change in time, let the right hand keep swinging without hitting the strings (a ghost strum), then play the new chord in time from the first beat of the next bar.

**Exercises that work**

| Exercise | Method | What to measure |
|---|---|---|
| One-minute changes | Pick two chords and switch between them, one strum per chord. Count the clean changes in 60 seconds | Write down the number and compare with yourself the next day (the starting number differs for each person) |
| One bar of metronome | 4 beats per chord at 60 bpm, then cut to 2 beats, then to 1 beat | The speed at which you change in time without mistakes |
| Four-chord loop | G C D Em, 8 rounds without stopping | Get through all eight rounds without losing the beat |

## Four-note chords and adding color to open chords

Once you can change triads smoothly, add chords that have a fourth note or that replace the third with another note. They let you play songs closer to the original.

The question of why the added notes give different moods is covered in the lesson "Chords: triads and seventh chords" of the music theory path. For now, remembering what you hear is enough.

- **7 (dominant)** is tense and wants to keep moving. Use it before returning to the home chord.
- **maj7** is soft and floating. Use it when you want warmth.
- **m7** is sad in a soft way, not as heavy as a plain minor chord.
- **sus2 / sus4** is neither major nor minor yet. It waits for an answer.
- **add9** is brighter and more open than a plain major chord.
