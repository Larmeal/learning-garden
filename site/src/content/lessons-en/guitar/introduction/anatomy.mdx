## Try naming the parts first

Pick up an acoustic guitar and point to these. If you do not have one nearby, use the picture below instead.

Point to a tuning machine, then move down the neck, past the fret wires, to the round hole in the body. Then point to the bar of wood under that hole where the strings are fixed. How many of these parts can you name without looking?

An acoustic guitar needs no electricity and no amp. All the sound comes from the wooden body, so each part has to do two jobs: hold the strings under tension, and help the sound become loud enough to hear. This page looks at both.

## Three big areas: head, neck, body

The parts in the picture fall into three big groups, and each group has its own main job.

| Area | Parts | Main job |
|---|---|---|
| **Head** (headstock) | headstock, tuning machines | Tune the strings to the right pitch |
| **Neck** | nut, neck, truss rod, fretboard, frets, inlays, heel | Decide which note each finger position gives, and carry the pull of the strings |
| **Body** | top, soundhole, rosette, bridge, saddle, pins, back, sides, bracing | Make the sound loud |

The strings run all the way from the body to the head, so they do not belong to one group.

When you press a string at a fret, the neck is the part you use. When you pluck and hear a loud sound without an amp, the body is the part at work. Most of the lessons on this path practice on the neck. The body is what makes one guitar sound different from another.

## How sound travels from the string to your ear

A plucked string is very quiet. Without the body of the guitar you can hardly hear it. The sound gets loud because the vibration of the string is passed through several parts, one after another. Press "Strum" to hear a synthesized E chord and watch the picture go through the steps, or choose a step yourself.

<AcousticAnatomy client:visible lang="en" mode="sound" />

In reality all five steps happen at the same time for as long as the string vibrates. The picture splits them up so that you can see where each part helps. The step you choose is drawn at full strength, and the earlier steps fade but stay on. Try steps 3 and 4 to see the wooden frame under the top, which the picture opens up for you.

<Callout type="note" title="Going deeper: why there is bracing">
A set of steel strings pulls toward the body with a total of tens of kilograms of force (the formula for string tension in the [Getting to know the guitar](/en/guitar/introduction/#how-a-guitar-makes-sound) lesson gives about 73 newtons for a single string). Almost all of that force ends up at the bridge, which is glued to the top.

The top has to be thin to vibrate easily, but if it were only thin it would bulge and crack under the pull. So the maker glues thin strips of wood under the top as a frame, which stops it from bulging and still lets the wood vibrate. The thicker the strips, the stiffer and stronger the top, but the quieter the guitar and the different its tone. A maker has to find the right balance in the bracing when designing the guitar.
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Challenge: hear and feel the body">
Try these three things on your acoustic guitar.

1. Pluck the open string 6 and rest the flat of your other hand on the top, on the opposite side of the bridge. Pluck again. You can feel the wood vibrate.
2. Pluck the open string 6 and cover the soundhole completely with your hand. The low sound goes away and the tone becomes dull. Take your hand off and compare again.
3. If you have a solid body electric guitar, pluck it without plugging it in. You hear only the quiet sound of the string, with no box to amplify it.
</Callout>

## How a classical guitar differs

The picture shows a steel-string acoustic guitar. A classical guitar, which uses nylon strings, has almost the same parts, but it differs in several places.

| Part | Steel-string | Classical (nylon-string) |
|---|---|---|
| headstock | A solid plate. The posts stand up from the face and the knobs are on the edge | A slotted headstock, with a long slot in the middle. The posts lie across the slot and the knobs are at the sides |
| nut | About 43 mm wide | Wider, about 52 mm, so the strings are further apart |
| truss rod | Yes | Often none, because nylon strings pull much less |
| bridge | Bridge pins hold the string ends | The strings are tied to the bridge directly with a knot (a tie block) |
| bracing | Usually X-bracing | Usually fan bracing, laid out like a fan, and lighter |
| pickguard | Often there, because the player strums with a pick | Often none, because the player mostly uses the fingers |

These differences are part of why the two kinds sound different, and why you should never put steel strings on a classical guitar. The much higher pull can damage the top or the bridge. An electric guitar has a different set of parts, such as pickups, a volume knob and a tremolo bar. You can see them in the diagram on the [Getting to know the guitar](/en/guitar/introduction/#the-parts-of-a-guitar) page.

## The parts to know before the other lessons

You do not need to memorize every name. Only a few parts come up often in the later lessons.

- The nut and the saddle are the two ends of the part of the string that can vibrate, and the distance between them is the scale length
- The fret and the fretboard are what the fretboard map lesson uses all the time
- The tuning machines are used every time you tune
- The truss rod and the saddle are the two places where action and intonation are adjusted, which a technician usually does in a setup

You will learn the rest as you need it. If you forget a name, come back and press it in the picture or look it up in the [Glossary](/en/guitar/introduction/glossary/).
