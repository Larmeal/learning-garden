## Try first, then look for the answer

Do these three things on your guitar, and listen.

1. Pluck the open string 6 and let it ring.
2. Use a fingertip to touch the string **lightly** right above the fret wire at fret 12 (do not press down so far that the string touches the fret), then pluck. Lift your finger off immediately after you pluck.
3. Repeat at fret 7, then at fret 5, and finally try fret 6.

The sound from steps 2 and 3 is clear, thin, like a bell, and higher than the open string. At fret 6 you get a dull sound that is not very loud. This clear sound is called a **harmonic**. The rest of this lesson explains why it is these frets.

</Step>

## One string vibrates in many ways at the same time

Think of a skipping rope tied at both ends. If you shake it slowly, the rope makes one big arc. If you shake it at exactly the right faster speed, the rope divides into two arcs, with a point in the middle that stays still. Faster again, and it divides into three arcs, four arcs, and so on.

A guitar string does the same. We name each pattern with a number k.

| k | The string divides into | Points that stay still | Frequency | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One arc (the whole string) | Only the two ends | 1× | The note of the string itself, called the **fundamental** |
| 2 | Two arcs | The middle (1/2) | 2× | Up 1 octave |
| 3 | Three arcs | 1/3 and 2/3 | 3× | Up 1 octave and a perfect 5th |
| 4 | Four arcs | 1/4, 2/4, 3/4 | 4× | Up 2 octaves |

A point that stays still is called a **node**. All of these vibration patterns happen at the same time every time you pluck. They mix into one sound that the ear hears, and k = 1 is the loudest, which is why we hear the note of that string.

</Step>

## See it: what does the lightly touching finger stop?

Below is one string. Each row is one vibration pattern (k = 1 to 6). The dashed line is the shape of that arc. The small round dots are the nodes that stay still. Choose the fret you touch at the top, then look at which rows "survive" (the dark line keeps moving) and which rows are "stopped".

<HarmonicsLab client:visible lang="en" start={12} />

**How to read the picture** A finger that touches lightly does not silence the string. It only forces that point of the string to stay still. A pattern that has a node exactly under the finger has no problem and keeps vibrating. A pattern that needs the point under the finger to move is stopped at once.

So when you choose fret 12, the patterns k = 1, 3, 5 are stopped, because they need the middle of the string to move. What is left is k = 2, 4, 6, which have a node exactly in the middle, and k = 2 is the lowest of the ones that are left. So it is the sound you hear: the same note, one octave higher.

</Step>

<Step>

## Why frets 12, 7 and 5

A harmonic can happen only when the finger touches a node of one of the patterns, and the nodes are at simple fractions of the string length (1/2, 1/3, 1/4 ...). The frets are placed by the formula 1 − 2^(−n/12), which happens to be close to those fractions in several places.

| Fret | Position (of the string length) | Close to the fraction | Patterns that survive | Sound you hear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 0.5000 | exactly 1/2 | k = 2, 4, 6 | Up 1 octave |
| 7 (and 19) | 0.3326 | 1/3 (0.3333) | k = 3, 6 | Up 1 octave + perfect 5th |
| 5 (and 24) | 0.2508 | 1/4 (0.2500) | k = 4 | Up 2 octaves |
| 4 and 9 | 0.2063 and 0.4054 | 1/5 and 2/5 | k = 5 (weak, because the error is larger) | Up 2 octaves + major 3rd (slightly out of tune) |
| 6, 8, 10 | 0.2929, 0.3700, 0.4388 | Not close to a simple fraction | None | A short, dull sound |

Go back to the picture above and choose frets 6, 8 and 10. No row survives, because no pattern has a node there. The finger stops the whole string.

Fret 12 is a special case and is the easiest to hear, because its position is exactly the middle, and the patterns that are stopped are k = 1, 3, 5, which are the loudest parts of the string. Frets 7 and 5 are close enough that the ear does not notice the error.

<Callout type="note" title="Going deeper: why fret 7 is not exactly 1/3">
The frets are placed so that every step is the same semitone (equal temperament). So the perfect 5th on the guitar is slightly narrower than the pure 5th (the ratio 3 to 2). The result is that the position of fret 7 is off from 1/3 by about 0.2%, or about half a millimeter on a 648 mm string. That is too small to hear. But if you touch exactly at 1/3, you get a harmonic that is a little purer.
</Callout>

## Playing technique to get the sound

A harmonic made by touching above the fret like this is called a **natural harmonic** (compare an artificial harmonic, where another finger makes a node at a different position, which is a more advanced technique). You get a clean sound with these rules.

- Touch **right above the fret wire**, not in the middle of the space between wires, because the node is exactly at the wire.
- Touch **lightly**. Just touch the string. Do not press until the string touches the wire, or you get an ordinary note.
- **Lift the finger off immediately** after you pluck. If the finger stays, it keeps stopping the string.
- Pluck near the bridge, or use the pickup nearest the bridge, to get a clearer harmonic, because plucking near the bridge wakes up more of the higher patterns.
- If the sound does not come out, move your finger a little closer to or further from the fret wire, a little at a time, until you find the clearest spot.

</Step>

## Using harmonics to tune and to check a guitar

**1. Checking intonation**

The harmonic at fret 12 is exactly the octave of the open string. Compare it with the note you get by really pressing fret 12. If they match, the intonation is good. If the pressed note is higher or lower than the harmonic, the saddle needs adjusting. That is a job for a technician or someone who knows how (see the glossary entries intonation and setup).

**2. Tuning with harmonics, without a tuner**

Harmonics let you compare two strings with a clear sound that does not blend together.

| Compare | Harmonic you get | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| String 6 fret 5 | k = 4 of E2 (82.41 Hz) | 329.6 Hz (E4) |
| String 5 fret 7 | k = 3 of A2 (110 Hz) | 330 Hz (E4) |

These two sounds are the same note (E4). Pluck them together and listen. If they are not yet in tune, you hear a pulsing "wow, wow" in a steady rhythm. Turn the tuning machine of string 5 a little at a time until the pulsing gets slower and disappears. The same method works for other pairs, but you do not need harmonics for every pair. Read the normal fret 5 method in the lesson "[Practice with a method](/en/guitar/practice-method/)".

The two frequencies are slightly different (329.6 and 330 Hz) because a harmonic in physics is an exact whole-number multiple (110 × 3 = 330), but the open strings are tuned by the equal temperament system (82.41 × 4 = 329.6). This small difference makes a very slow "wow" pulse even when the tuning is right. It is good for rough tuning. If you need the highest accuracy, use a tuner.

**3. The sound in music**

Harmonics are used as bell sounds or special sounds at the start of a song and in links between sections. They are also the basis of the **pinch harmonic** technique (touching the string with the thumb right after the pick plucks it, to make a high squealing sound) on electric guitar.

**Try more yourself**

1. Tune string 5 against string 6 with the harmonics at fret 5 and fret 7, then compare with a tuner.
2. Play the fret 12 harmonic on every string (string 6 to string 1) and listen to check that every string gives the same note as its open string, one octave higher.
3. Try to find the harmonics at fret 4 and fret 9 and see if they are easy or hard to hear (it varies, because they are weaker).

</Step>
