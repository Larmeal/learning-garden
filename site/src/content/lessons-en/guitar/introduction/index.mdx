## How a guitar makes sound

The guitar is a plucked string instrument. A plucked string vibrates at a certain frequency, and the frequency is the pitch. The sound you hear comes from three parts.

1. **The string vibrates.** This makes a very quiet sound.
2. **The amplifier.** An acoustic guitar uses its wooden body as a sound box. An electric guitar uses a pickup to turn the vibration into an electric signal, and sends it to an amp to make it louder.
3. **The player.** You press the string at a fret to change the pitch. You pluck harder or softer to change the volume and the tone.

**Why does pressing a fret give a higher sound?** The frequency of a string depends on three things. The shorter the string, the higher the sound. The tighter the string, the higher the sound. The thicker the string, the lower the sound. Pressing a fret makes the part of the string that can vibrate shorter, so the sound is higher. The frets are placed so that each step goes up by exactly one semitone.

<Callout type="note" title="Going deeper: the numbers behind the frets">
The length of string that can vibrate (the scale length) times 2 to the power of minus n over twelve gives the length that is left after pressing fret n. So the distance from the nut to fret n is L × (1 − 2^(−n/12)). At fret 12 exactly half of the string is left (the sound goes up one octave).

| scale length | fret 1 | fret 5 | fret 7 | fret 12 | fret 19 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25.5 in (Fender style) | 1.43 in | 6.40 in | 8.48 in | 12.75 in | 16.99 in |
| 24.75 in (Gibson style) | 1.39 in | 6.21 in | 8.23 in | 12.38 in | 16.49 in |

So the frets get closer together by a constant ratio. This matches what you see on the fretboard map in this path.
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Challenge: string tension">
A string carries a tension given by T = μ × (2 × L × f)², where μ is the mass per unit length of the string, L is the scale length and f is the frequency. A string 1 of gauge .010 in (steel), at a scale length of 25.5 in and the note E4 (329.63 Hz), has a tension of about 73 newtons (about 16 pounds-force). That is one string alone. Try to calculate it yourself and check against this value. Then think about why changing the string gauge also means adjusting the truss rod and the intonation.
</Callout>

## The three big families

| Type | Strings | Sound | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Classical** (nylon-string) | Nylon | Soft and warm, rounder and quieter | Classical music, flamenco, bossa nova, and beginners who do not want sore fingertips |
| **Acoustic** (steel-string) | Steel | Bright, loud and sparkling | Strumming and singing, folk, country, pop |
| **Electric** | Steel, connected to an amp | Many tones, from clean to distorted | Rock, blues, jazz, metal, and styles that need effects |

All types are tuned the same way (E A D G B E), and the same chord shapes work on all of them. Choose the type that matches the music you want to play, because motivation matters more than specifications. The details, the sub-types and how to choose are on the sub-page "[Types of guitars](/en/guitar/introduction/types/)".

## Why this path uses English terms

Almost all guitar vocabulary comes from English, and guitarists in many countries use these words as they are, for example fret, bend, capo and riff. This path writes these words in English and does not translate them. The reason is that you will need them when you search for teaching videos, read tab, buy equipment, or talk to other musicians. Translating them would make you learn everything twice.

**The rules of this path**

- A new term gets a short explanation the first time it appears in a lesson.
- Every term in the glossary says which lesson teaches it.
- When you meet a word you do not know in another lesson, open the glossary at any time. Try it now, and search for "capo", "bend" and "intonation" as a warm-up.

### How to read tab

Tab is a diagram with six lines for the six strings. The top line is string 1 (the highest sound). A number on a line is the fret to press. The number 0 means an open string. Read from left to right.

```text
e|-------------------|
B|-------------------|
G|-------------------|
D|-------0---2---3---|
A|-------------------|
E|-------------------|
```

This example is the open D string (D), then fret 2 (E), then fret 3 (F).

## Getting ready before the first lesson

**What you need**

- One guitar that you can play (it does not have to be expensive, but the strings should not be so high that pressing hurts your hand)
- A tuner (a phone app is fine)
- A metronome app
- 2 or 3 picks of different sizes
- A notebook for your practice (a practice log)

**Time** Practicing 30 minutes a day is better than practicing three hours once a week. The first lesson has a practice schedule.

**If your hand hurts** In the first few weeks your fingertips will hurt and slowly get harder. This is normal. Pain in the wrist or forearm is not normal. Stop and read the lesson "[Posture and hand technique](/en/guitar/technique/)".
