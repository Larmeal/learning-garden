## Reading history so that it helps you

The years and names below are approximate, as they are commonly told in the history of this instrument. They were not checked against sources when this page was written. If you want to cite them, compare them with a book or a museum first. The goal of this page is to see the **reasons** the guitar changed shape, because those reasons still explain what we play every day, for example why open chords have the shapes they have, and why there are two kinds of pickup.

Two problems come back again and again in this history.

1. **Not loud enough**, when the guitar has to play with a big band or in a large room.
2. **Wanting a new sound**, when the next generation of musicians wants a sound that does not exist yet.

</Step>

<Step>

## Before the guitar: thousands of years of plucked strings

Instruments with strings stretched over a neck and a body existed long before the guitar. Examples are the **lute** in Europe and the **oud** in the Arab world, which are the ancestors most often named in history. The resonating body and the neck with stretched strings are ideas that were passed down to the guitar.

In the Middle Ages and the Renaissance (about the 15th to 16th century), Spain had instruments called the **vihuela** and the **guitarra**, which are the closest ancestors. They used strings in pairs (courses), so they had four or five courses instead of the six single strings of today.

These instruments were played in the royal court and in the street alike. This is where the guitar's use for accompanying songs and dance music began, and that is still its main job today.

</Step>

<Step>

## The 19th century: the shapes we still use

**Antonio de Torres Jurado**, a Spanish guitar maker (middle to late 19th century), is credited with setting the design of the modern **classical** guitar: a larger body, fan bracing under the top, and a scale length of about 650 mm that is still the standard today.

On the other side of the world, **C. F. Martin**, a guitar maker who moved from Germany to the United States, set up a workshop and developed the X-shaped top bracing (**X-bracing**), which can stand the pull of **steel strings**. The result was the steel-string acoustic guitar, louder and brighter than nylon, which became the core of folk, country and blues.

**The important reason** Steel strings are louder but also tighter, so the guitar body had to be stronger. The whole structure of the acoustic guitar that we see today is an answer to this problem.

</Step>

<Step>

## Early 20th century: it has to be louder

When the guitar had to play with banjos, trumpets and wind instruments (early jazz bands), its sound was not loud enough. There were two solutions.

- **Archtop**: a body with an arched top like a violin, and f-shaped sound holes, which works in a jazz band. It was developed by **Orville Gibson** and the Gibson company (founded in 1902).
- **Resonator guitar**: metal cones fitted inside the guitar body to make the sound louder, for example the brands **National** and **Dobro** (late 1920s). They give a loud, clear metallic sound that is still the typical sound of slide blues.

The guitar could play both chords and melodies, but it had reached the point where nothing could make it louder than a wooden box.

</Step>

<Step>

## The 1930s to the 1950s: electricity

The final answer to the problem "not loud enough" was the **pickup**, which picks up the vibration of the string as an electric signal and sends it into an amp. In the 1930s makers such as **Rickenbacker** sold some of the first electric guitars (of the lap steel type), and Gibson followed with electric archtop guitars.

The next step was the **solid body**: a guitar made of solid wood with no resonating box, because the sound comes from the pickup, not from the wood of the body. The advantage is that there is no **feedback** at high volume.

| Approximate year | Event |
|---|---|
| About 1950 | **Fender** released the first mass-produced solid body guitar (later called the **Telecaster**) |
| 1952 | The **Gibson Les Paul** went on sale (the guitarist Les Paul helped to develop it) |
| 1954 | The **Fender Stratocaster** came with three pickups and a tremolo arm |
| About 1955 to 1957 | Gibson's **humbucker**, which cancels the hum of the single-coil |

The sound of this period is the origin of the terms **Strat sound** (clear, bright, sharp) and **Les Paul sound** (thick, warm, long sustain), two opposites that guitarists still use for comparison.

</Step>

<Step>

## The 1960s to the 1980s: loud and distorted

An **amp** that distorts became part of the sound. Players in Britain and America turned tube amps up until they began to distort. At first this was seen as a mistake, but it became the standard sound of **rock**. Amp makers such as **Marshall** (starting about 1962) designed amps to distort on purpose. Musicians added **effect pedals** such as fuzz, wah and phaser to make new sounds.

In the late 1970s came the **locking tremolo** (Floyd Rose), which keeps the strings in tune even when the arm is used hard, and the **superstrat**, a guitar shape adapted for fast playing (brands such as Ibanez). These led to the shred era in hard rock and metal.

</Step>

<Step>

## The 1990s to today: digital

Technology changed the way guitarists get their sound.

- **Modeling amp**: simulates the sound of many amps and speaker cabinets with digital circuits. It started in the 1990s, and today the sound is good enough to record music that is sold in shops.
- **Home recording**: you play into a computer through an audio interface and use plugins instead of an amp, so you can practice quietly.
- **Extended range**: 7-string and 8-string guitars for the low notes in metal and progressive music.
- **Online video lessons** let people learn guitar anywhere in the world. They can learn from video, transcribe songs and share their own playing at almost no cost.

<Callout type="note" title="What history shows us">
Every period of the history is the result of musicians who wanted to be heard, or wanted a sound that did not exist yet. It was not only inventors designing on purpose. The distortion of an amp was a mistake before it became a style. This is why you will see that the "theory" in this path always comes after what musicians actually do.
</Callout>

**A message for people who learn by ear** All of this history happened because people listened and tried things, not because of textbooks. You do not need to feel that learning by ear is wrong. The goal of this path is to add language and structure to what your ear already knows.

</Step>
