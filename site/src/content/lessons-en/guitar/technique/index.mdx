## Why players who learn by ear have technique habits to check

People who never had lessons usually build a posture that "works" by themselves. It is not always wrong, but three habits stop you from playing faster or for a long time: pressing harder than needed, a locked right wrist, and flat fingers that touch the neighboring strings.

This lesson does not ask you to change how you play. Use it as a checklist. Try one item at a time on something you already play. What you get is a cleaner sound with less force.

Record a short video of yourself playing a familiar song for 30 seconds. Film the left hand from the front and the right hand from the side. Keep it, and compare after you finish this lesson.
</Callout>

## Sitting and holding the guitar

- **Sit up straight on a chair without armrests.** Rest the guitar on your right thigh (for a right-handed player) with the body of the guitar against your torso.
- **Tilt the neck up a little.** It should not point at the floor and should not be exactly parallel to it. This keeps the left wrist from bending sharply.
- **Rest the right forearm on the edge of the body.** Let the arm hang without raising the shoulder.
- **When standing,** adjust the strap so that the guitar sits at the same height as when you sit, and the neck does not drop. Many players hang the guitar low for the look and then have to bend the left wrist much more.

A quick check: neither shoulder should be raised, you should breathe normally, and if you suddenly let go of the neck with the left hand, the guitar should not move or turn.

</Step>

## Left hand: press just enough, not as hard as you can

There is only one principle: the note sounds clear when the string just touches the fret, not when you press with all your strength.

1. **Press close to the fret wire.** The fingertip should be just on the near side of the fret wire, not on top of the wire and not in the middle of the space. The closer to the wire, the less force you need.
2. **Use the fingertip, not the pad.** Curve the finger as if holding a small ball, so the pad does not touch the lower string that must ring or the upper string that must stay silent.
3. **Put the thumb behind the neck,** around the index and middle fingers. Do not squeeze. It only resists the pressing force. (A hooked thumb over the top of the neck works for some playing, such as bending strings or fretting string 6 with the thumb, but it should not be your starting position for everything.)
4. **One finger per fret.** In one position, the index finger takes the first fret, the middle finger the next, then the ring finger, then the little finger. This keeps the hand from jumping around.
5. **Keep lifted fingers close to the strings.** Fingers that are not fretting should not float high. Every time one goes high, you lose time bringing it back.

Test your pressure: pluck one note you can hear clearly. Then slowly ease off until the sound starts to buzz. Press back just a little until the buzz goes away. That force is the force you really need.

</Step>

## Right hand: holding the pick and alternate picking

**Holding the pick.** Pinch the pick between the tip of the thumb and the side of the index finger, with the tip of the pick sticking out about two or three millimeters. Hold it just firmly enough that it does not slip. Do not squeeze. A tight grip makes the wrist and forearm tense too.

**Alternate picking.** Pick down on the main beat and up on the offbeat ("&"), even when the notes are the same. The advantage is that the right hand swings with the same rhythm all the time, which improves speed and steadiness (the same principle as in the lesson "[Rhythm and Strumming](/en/guitar/rhythm/#the-right-hand-is-a-clock)").

**Where the movement comes from.** Mostly from rotating the wrist (like turning a door handle) plus a little forearm rotation. It is not bending the wrist up and down, and you should not move the whole arm.

**Pick angle.** If the pick is tilted slightly against the string, it slides past the string more smoothly. A pick that is square to the string catches on the string when you play fast.

<Callout type="tip" title="Pick thickness">
A thin pick is good for strumming chords. A heavier pick (about 0.88 to 1.2 mm and up) gives a sharper single-note sound and is easier to control. There is no number that is right for everyone. Try a few and choose the one that keeps your right hand most relaxed.
</Callout>

</Step>

## Basic exercise: Spider

This exercise is an example of the "one technique" slot in the practice schedule of the first lesson. It trains three things at once: independent fingers, alternate picking and changing strings.

Fingers 1 2 3 4 are the index, middle, ring and little fingers, placed on frets 5 6 7 8 in that order. Play from string 6 up to string 1, then come back down.

```text
E|-5-6-7-8---------------------
A|-----------5-6-7-8-----------
D|---------------------5-6-7-8-
G|-----------------------------
B|-----------------------------
e|-----------------------------
```

(This example shows the first three strings used. Continue the same way up to string 1.)

How to practice

1. Set the metronome to 60 bpm and play one note per beat, alternate picking: down, up, down, up.
2. Every time you change string, take care that the picking direction does not leave the alternating pattern.
3. If you play three clean rounds, raise it to 66 bpm (following the rule of the first lesson).
4. Change the finger order to 1-3-2-4, 4-3-2-1 and 1-2-4-3 so that the weaker fingers get to work.

Listen for every note being equally loud and starting exactly on the beat. Watch whether the fingers you are not using float close to the strings.

</Step>

## Strange sounds: read them from the symptom

| Symptom | Common cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Buzz (string vibrating against the fret) | Pressing far from the fret wire, too little force, or the finger not square to the string | Move the fingertip closer to the wire, check the finger angle |
| Dead sound, no sustain | The pad or tip of another finger touches a string that should ring | Curve the finger joint more |
| Strings you did not pluck vibrate | An open string was touched and keeps ringing, or the right hand touched it | Learn to mute the unused strings (lesson "[Rhythm and Strumming](/en/guitar/rhythm/#muting-makes-the-rhythm-sharp)") |
| Pick scrapes or catches on the string | Pick square to the string, pick sticking out too far | Tilt the pick slightly, let less of the pick stick out |
| Tension in the left or right wrist | Gripping too tight, pressing more than needed | Reduce the pressure with the test above, rest, then play slower than before |
| Little finger will not reach the fret | The hand is twisted, so the other fingers tilt | Turn the left elbow slightly toward the body so that all four fingers are square to the neck |

Go back to the video you recorded and compare it with this list. If one thing is clearly visible, fix only that one this week.
