## A scale is a pattern, not a set of notes

A **scale** is seven notes in order of pitch, inside one octave. What makes it that scale is the pattern of distances between the notes. The major scale is the main scale. We use it to count and name all the other scales and chords in Western music.

**The formula**: the distance from each note to the next is

**whole step · whole step · half step · whole step · whole step · whole step · half step**
**W W H W W W H**

In C major (the white keys only) the two half steps fall at E–F and B–C, as [lesson 1](/en/music-theory/pitch-notation/) said. This is why the white keys starting from C sound major. When you start from another note, you must raise or lower some notes to keep the pattern W W H W W W H.

## How to build a scale from any note

Two rules work together.

1. **Use all seven letters, each one once.** If you start from D, you need D E F G A B C in this order (each letter may have a sharp or a flat).
2. **Adjust with sharps or flats to get the distances of the formula W W H W W W H.**

Example: D major

| Degree | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Letter | D | E | F | G | A | B | C |
| Distance from the previous degree | – | W | W | H | W | W | W |
| Result | D | E | F♯ | G | A | B | C♯ |

F must be raised to F♯ so that E→F♯ is a whole step. C must be raised to C♯ so that B→C♯ is a whole step and C♯→D is a half step. This is why D major has two sharps.

**Quick check**: the half steps must always fall at degrees 3–4 and 7–8. If they do not, the scale is wrong.

## Key signatures and the circle of fifths

A key is the home of a song. A song in D major mostly uses the notes of the D major scale. The **key signature** is the sharps or flats written at the start of the staff, so that we do not need to write them again on every note.

If you line up the keys, going up one **perfect 5th** each time, the number of sharps grows by one each time (C → G → D → A → E → B → F♯). Going down one perfect 5th (or up one perfect 4th), the number of flats grows by one each time (C → F → B♭ → E♭ → A♭ → D♭). The keys form a circle. This is the **circle of fifths**.

- Sharps are added in the order **F C G D A E B** (Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle).
- Flats are added in the order **B E A D G C F** (the reverse of the order above).
- The last sharp in a key signature is a half step below the root (in the key of D: C♯ comes just before D). You can use this to find the key from the signature.
- The second-to-last flat is the name of the key (in keys with more than one flat).

## Relative keys and the names of the degrees

The inner circle shows the **relative minor** of each major key. This is the minor key that uses the same notes but starts from degree 6 (A minor is the relative minor of C major). The two keys have the same key signature (the next lesson explains how the center of the music moves).

**The names of the degrees.** Two sets of names are common. This table gives the names by function.

| Degree | Name by function | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | tonic | Home. The most still. |
| 2 | supertonic | Moving. |
| 3 | mediant | Decides major or minor. |
| 4 | subdominant | Leaves home gently. |
| 5 | dominant | Tense. Wants to go home. |
| 6 | submediant | An alternative home (the minor side). |
| 7 | leading tone | Wants to rise to the tonic (a half step away). |

Degrees 5 and 7 are the two notes that create the strongest need to return to degree 1. This is the base of the harmony in the next lessons.

**On the guitar.** When you know that a song is in G major (one sharp, F♯), you know at once that all the notes of the song come from G A B C D E F♯. The major scale shapes in the guitar path, in the lesson "Scales on the fretboard", are just this same formula laid out on the neck.
