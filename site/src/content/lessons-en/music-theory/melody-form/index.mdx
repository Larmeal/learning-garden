## Melody and chords are one thing

A melody that sounds good is usually tied to the chords that support it. The listener feels both at the same time, even without knowing it. This lesson goes from a single note to the form of a whole song, using what you learned in all the earlier lessons.

**The notes of a melody fall into two groups, depending on the chord that is playing**

1. **Chord tone**: a note that is in the chord at that moment (over C: C E G). It feels still and "right".
2. **Non-chord tone**: a note that is not in the chord. It creates tension, and it relaxes when it moves to a chord tone.

Common kinds of non-chord tone:

| Kind | What it does | Example over C (C E G) |
|---|---|---|
| **Passing tone** | passes from one chord tone to another, in a continuous step motion | C **D** E |
| **Neighbor tone** | goes one step away and comes back | E **F** E |
| **Suspension** | holds a note of the old chord over the new chord, then relaxes down | hold F from an F chord, then relax to E over C |
| **Appoggiatura** | leaps to an unstable note on a strong beat, then relaxes down by step | D (strong) → C |

A rule you can use right away: **put non-chord tones on weak beats and chord tones on strong beats**. A typical melody follows this when it wants to feel still, and breaks it when it wants to feel tense. This matches what you practice in the guitar path, lesson "[Improvisation](/en/guitar/improvisation/)", about landing on a chord tone when the chord changes.

</Step>

## The notes of the scale have a pull

In a major key, each step tends to move to a neighboring step that is more stable.

| Step | Feeling | Tendency |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | home | stays |
| 2 | unstable | down to 1 |
| 3 | fairly stable | stays, or down to 2 / 1 |
| 4 | unstable | down to 3 |
| 5 | stable, supporting | stays |
| 6 | fairly stable | up to 7 or down to 5 |
| 7 | the most tense | up a half step to 1 |

So a melody uses **7 → 1, 4 → 3, 2 → 1** to make an ending, and uses steps 1, 3, 5 (the tonic triad) as places to rest. Look at the last phrase of any song and you will find this motion almost every time (for example, the end of "Happy Birthday", "... to you", goes down to 1).

**How to listen with numbers**: hum the melody and ask "what step is the last note?" If it is step 1, it sounds completely finished. If it is 2 or 5, it still feels like it continues.

</Step>

## Motifs and phrases

A melody that is easy to remember usually has a structure that can be repeated. It is built from small units.

- **Motif**: a short idea of 2 to 5 notes with a recognizable shape (for example the first 4 notes of Beethoven's 5th: three repeated notes, then a fall of a major third).
- **Phrase**: a musical sentence about 2 or 4 bars long, ending with a small cadence.
- **Period**: two phrases that form a question and an answer. The first phrase ends on a half cadence (the question). The second phrase ends on an authentic cadence (the answer).

Simple ways to develop a motif:

1. **Repeat**: exactly the same.
2. **Repeat on another step (sequence)**: the same shape, moved up or down by steps of the scale.
3. **Change the rhythm**: the same notes, a new rhythm.
4. **Change the ending**: repeat, then end on a different note to make an answer.
5. **Inversion**: the same shape turned upside down, going up where it went down.

Most melodies that stick in the head do not use many ideas. They use one idea, repeated and developed cleverly.

</Step>

## Ordering chords so the notes move as little as possible (voice leading)

When you play chords one after another, each note in the chord should jump as little as possible, and notes that repeat should be "held". This makes the sound connect smoothly.

For example, C → Am → F → G on the guitar. If you use all open shapes, the top note jumps around. If you choose shapes that are close together on the neck, the notes move less.

Four rules:

1. **Hold the notes that repeat** (C → Am has C and E in common).
2. **Notes that do not repeat move step by step** if possible.
3. **The bass may jump**, because the bass is usually the root.
4. **The top voice should be a singable melody.** The top voice is the part the listener follows most.

**On the guitar**: this is why CAGED (guitar path, lesson "[Barre chords and the CAGED system](/en/guitar/barre-caged/)") is useful. You choose the shape of the next chord that is close to the current shape on the neck, instead of jumping back to an open shape.

</Step>

## Song form

Almost all songs are built from sections that repeat. Common forms:

| Form | Short form | Description | Found in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Verse – Chorus | V C V C B C | the verse tells the story, the chorus is the core of the song, the bridge changes the view | pop, rock, country |
| AABA (32-bar) | A A B A | A repeats, B (the bridge) is different, then A returns | jazz standards, older songs |
| 12-bar blues | – | 12 bars that loop I–IV–V | blues, rock and roll |
| Strophic | A A A | the same tune repeated with new words | folk songs, hymns |

**Typical length**: phrases of 4 bars, put together into sections of 8 or 16 bars.

**What each section usually does**

- Verse: plain chords, a narrow melody range, tells the story.
- Pre-chorus: adds tension, often ends on V (half cadence) to prepare the chorus.
- Chorus: higher pitch, clear chords, repeated, catchy.
- Bridge: changes the chords or the key, uses chords not yet used (for example vi, ii) or borrowed chords.

Transcribe a song you like into a map of sections and chords as numbers, and you will see these forms clearly (the method is in the guitar path, lesson "[Learning songs by ear and building a repertoire](/en/guitar/ear-songs/)").

</Step>

## Try writing eight bars

Use what you learned from every lesson in this path to write a short song in 30 minutes.

1. **Choose a key and a tempo.** Suggested: G major, 4/4, 90 bpm.
2. **Choose eight bars of chords.** Try this: bars 1 to 4 are G | Em | C | D (ends on V). Bars 5 to 8 are G | Em | C and D (half a bar each) | G (ends on I).
3. **Make bar 4 the question and bar 8 the answer.** A half cadence at bar 4 (D), followed by an authentic cadence at bars 7 to 8 (D → G).
4. **Think of a motif of 3 to 4 notes** from the G major pentatonic (G A B D E), ending on a chord tone of that bar.
5. **Repeat the motif in bars 2 and 3** as a sequence or with a changed rhythm.
6. **Check the endings.** Bar 4 should end on a note of D (D, F♯ or A) and bar 8 should end on G (step 1).
7. **Record it.** Play the chords together with the melody. Listen for places where a non-chord tone falls on a strong beat. Fix one of them.

<Callout type="tip" title="Note">
You do not need lyrics or a big idea. The result does not have to be good. The goal is to use everything you learned (key, chords, functions, phrase structure) as deliberate decisions, not accidents.
</Callout>

When you finish this path, go back to the songs you have played for ten years and try to explain them in the language you learned. You will find that everything your ear has always known now has a name.

</Step>
