## When you meet a chord that is not in the key

The seven chords from [the last lesson](/en/music-theory/diatonic-harmony/) explain almost all simple songs. But good songs have unusual chords that still do not sound out of place. The reason is that songs borrow chords from somewhere else, for a good reason. There are three common sources.

1. **Secondary dominant**: the dominant of another chord in the key (not of I)
2. **Modal interchange**: borrowing from the parallel minor (or another mode of the same tonic)
3. **Modulation**: a real change of key, temporary or permanent

When you meet a chord that is not in the key, ask in this order: is it the dominant of the next chord? Is it a chord from the parallel minor? If it is neither, it may be a modulation.

</Step>

## Secondary dominant: the dominant of a neighbor

A V7 chord pulls strongly back to I because of its tritone ([lesson 7](/en/music-theory/diatonic-harmony/)). The same mechanism works on **any other major or minor chord in the key** (not vii°, because it is not stable enough to be a target). Put the V7 of that chord in front of it, and that chord feels like home for a moment.

This is written V7/x and read "five of x".

| Name | In C major | Goes to |
|---|---|---|
| V7/ii | **A7** | Dm |
| V7/iii | **B7** | Em |
| V7/IV | **C7** | F |
| V7/V | **D7** | G |
| V7/vi | **E7** | Am |

A familiar example: **C – A7 – Dm – G7 – C** (I – V7/ii – ii – V7 – I). A7 contains C♯, which is not in C major, but it pulls up to the D of Dm like a leading tone (a half step). It adds a flavor that makes the song feel like it travels more.

**How to know it is a secondary dominant**: the chord is major or dominant 7, and its root is a perfect 5th above the root of the next chord. For example E7 → Am (E is a P5 above A). If the next chord is not like that, it is not a secondary dominant of that chord.

</Step>

## ii–V–I: the skeleton of jazz and later songs

The sequence **ii – V – I** is predominant → dominant → tonic. It is the most common chord movement in jazz, because the roots of the three chords fall by a perfect 5th each time (D → G → C). Every move is "dominant → tonic".

In C major with seventh chords: **Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7**

| Chord | Notes | Observe |
|---|---|---|
| Dm7 | D F A C | |
| G7 | G B D F | The C in Dm7 falls a half step to B, F stays |
| Cmaj7 | C E G B | F falls a half step to E, B is held |

Almost every note moves by no more than a step or stays where it is. That is smooth **voice leading** ([next lesson](/en/music-theory/melody-form/)), and it is why the sound feels so smooth.

**ii–V–I can move its target**: Gm7 – C7 – Fmaj7 is the ii–V–I of the key of F, and it can be inserted into a song in C (the C7 here is V7/IV from the table above).

</Step>

## Borrowing chords from the parallel minor (modal interchange)

A major key and a minor key with the same tonic (C major and C minor) are like siblings. A major song can borrow chords from C minor.

C minor (natural) has the chords Cm, Ddim, **E♭**, **Fm**, Gm, **A♭**, **B♭**. When they are borrowed into a C major song, the most common ones are:

| Borrowed chord | Called | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| **Fm** (iv) | minor four | "a little bitter", at the end of a phrase before going back to C, heard in many ballads |
| **B♭** (♭VII) | flat seven | open rock sound |
| **A♭** (♭VI) | flat six | grand, like a film soundtrack |
| **E♭** (♭III) | flat three | dark, solid |

A familiar example: **C – F – Fm – C** (I – IV – iv – I). The chord F changes to Fm, so the A (the 3rd of F) is lowered to A♭. The bright sound turns dark for a moment, then returns to bright.

**On the guitar**: in the guitar path, lesson "Improvisation", if you play the minor pentatonic over a major song, the sound of bending the ♭3 up toward the major 3rd is the same idea: borrowing a color from the minor into the major.

</Step>

## Changing key (modulation)

**Modulation** means moving the tonic to a new note and staying there long enough that the ear accepts it as the new home (unlike a secondary dominant, which is only a short visit). There are two common ways.

1. **Through a shared chord (pivot chord)**: choose a chord that belongs to both keys and reinterpret it. For example, from C major to G major: Am is vi in C and ii in G. Play C – Am – D – G (Am is vi of C, then becomes ii of G, followed by V–I of G).
2. **A direct key change**: jump straight into the last section of the song. It is popular to raise the key by a half step or a whole step, to add excitement in the last chorus (the "truck driver's gear change").

The closest keys are the keys next to each other on the circle of fifths (for example C to G or F), because they share 6 of 7 notes. The farthest key is on the opposite side of the circle (C and F♯).

</Step>

## Bass as a line: connecting chords

Chords that change do not all need to be played with the root. A bass that walks in steps makes the harmony flow much more smoothly.

- **Walking down the scale**: C – G/B – Am – Am/G – F (the bass is C B A G F). This pattern is found in many ballads and folk songs.
- **Pedal point**: the bass stays on the tonic while the chords above change. You get a tension that is followed by a release.

Slash chords ([lesson 6](/en/music-theory/diatonic-harmony/), [chords](/en/music-theory/chords/)) are the tool used to write these.

<Callout type="try" title="Practice">
1. In G major, write V7/ii, V7/V and V7/vi (answer: E7, A7, B7; check with the rule "root a P5 above the target").
2. Play C – Em – Am – F, then change Em to E7 (C – E7 – Am – F). Listen to how E7 pulls toward Am more clearly.
3. Play C – F – Fm – C on the guitar. Listen for the place where A drops to A♭.
</Callout>
