## Seven steps, seven chords

Stack notes two steps apart (from [the last lesson](/en/music-theory/chords/#a-chord-is-notes-stacked-in-thirds)) on every step of the major scale, using only the notes of that scale, and you get seven chords. They are called the **diatonic** chords of the key. The pattern is the same in every key.

| Step | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Type | major | minor | minor | major | major | minor | diminished |
| In C | C | Dm | Em | F | G | Am | Bdim |
| In G | G | Am | Bm | C | D | Em | F♯dim |
| In E | E | F♯m | G♯m | A | B | C♯m | D♯dim |

When you stack four notes (seventh chords) in the key of C, you get Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, **G7**, Am7, Bm7♭5. Notice that the dominant seventh chord appears on only one step of a major key, the V. This is where its own special tension comes from.

**Roman numerals** use capital letters for major (I IV V) and lower case for minor (ii iii vi). A small circle means diminished (vii°). Almost every song in a major key uses mainly I IV V vi ii. The iii and vii° are used less.

</Step>

## Changing key with numbers

Professional musicians talk in numbers, because numbers move to another key instantly. The most common chord set in pop music is I–V–vi–IV.

| Key | I | V | vi | IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | C | G | Am | F |
| G | G | D | Em | C |
| A | A | E | F♯m | D |
| E | E | B | C♯m | A |
| B♭ | B♭ | F | Gm | E♭ |

The **Nashville system** writes the same idea with Arabic numerals (1 4 5 6m). It is widely used in studios and bands because it is fast to write and the key can be decided later ("this song is in G" → 1 = G, 4 = C, 5 = D).

**What you really gain from using numbers**

- You can move a song to fit a singer's range right away.
- You see that different songs use the same skeleton, so you can transcribe faster because you can guess the next chord.
- Your chord charts get shorter.

## Chord functions

The seven chords are not equal. The ear hears them doing different jobs. They fall into three groups.

| Function | Chords | Feeling |
|---|---|---|
| **Tonic (T)** | I (with vi and iii as substitutes) | home, still, finished |
| **Subdominant / Predominant (S)** | IV, ii | leaving, open |
| **Dominant (D)** | V, vii° | tense, wants to go home |

The normal motion of harmony is **T → S → D → T**. Going backwards (D → S), for example I–V–IV, is also found in rock and blues. It feels freer and does not lead to an ending. This is why I–IV–V–I sounds like a complete sentence.

**Why V wants to go back to I**: G7 (V7 of C) contains B and F, which are a tritone apart, an interval that is not stable. B wants to rise a half step to C, and F wants to fall a half step to E. Each note moves only a little, and together they land on the notes of C major (C E G). This motion is the pull that makes V–I the strongest closing phrase.

</Step>

## Cadences

At the end of a musical line there are usually two chords that work like punctuation.

| Name | Chords | Like | Example in C |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Authentic** | V → I (or V7 → I) | a full stop, a complete ending | G → C |
| **Plagal** | IV → I | the "amen", a soft ending | F → C |
| **Half** | ends on V | a comma, not finished | ... → G |
| **Deceptive** | V → vi | a trick, not what you expected | G → Am |

Listen to songs you like. The end of a section is usually one of these four. If a section ends on V and the next section starts, it is not finished. If it goes V → I, it is finished.

</Step>

## Minor keys

The chords of natural minor (A minor = A B C D E F G):

| Step | i | ii° | ♭III | iv | v | ♭VI | ♭VII |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| In A minor | Am | Bdim | C | Dm | Em | F | G |

In most music **the v is changed to V** (E or E7 instead of Em). It borrows the note G♯ from harmonic minor, to get a leading tone that pulls back to i. This is why you often see **i – iv – V** in minor (Am – Dm – E), which is the same as I – IV – V in major.

A very common set in minor rock is **i – ♭VI – ♭III – ♭VII** (Am – F – C – G). It is the same as vi – IV – I – V of C major, because they use the same set of notes (relative keys). This is why I–V–vi–IV sounds sad when it starts on vi: it has become minor.

**Finding the key from the chords you see**: if a song uses three major chords that are neighbors on the circle of fifths (for example C G D or D A E), the middle one is I, and the other two are IV and V.

**On the guitar**: the guitar path, lesson "[Learning songs by ear and building a repertoire](/en/guitar/ear-songs/)", uses these numbers when transcribing songs, so that they can be moved to any key.
