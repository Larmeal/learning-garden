## Beat, tempo and measure

Rhythm starts with something most people can clap or tap a foot to. It is called the **beat**. The speed of the beat is the **tempo**, measured in bpm (beats per minute). 60 bpm is one beat per second.

The beats do not all have the same weight. They are arranged in repeating groups, and each group is called a **measure** (or **bar**). The first beat of a measure is usually the strongest, and this helps the ear hear the group. A vertical line on the sheet music separates the measures.

**Common groups of beats** are 4 beats (strong, weak, medium, weak), 3 beats (strong, weak, weak, like a waltz) and 2 beats (strong, weak, like a march).

</Step>

<Step>

## The time signature

Two numbers stacked at the start of a piece, such as 4/4, tell you how the beats are grouped.

- **Top number**: how many beats are in one measure.
- **Bottom number**: which note counts as one beat (4 = quarter note, 8 = eighth note).

4/4 means "four beats, one quarter note per beat". It is the most common in pop, rock and blues. It is sometimes written as the letter C. 3/4 is found in waltzes and some slow songs.

**Note values**: each type is half as long as the one before it.

| Name (US) | Name (UK) | Length in beats (in x/4) | Matching rest |
|---|---|---|---|
| whole note | semibreve | 4 | a whole-measure rest |
| half note | minim | 2 | a two-beat rest |
| quarter note | crotchet | 1 | a one-beat rest |
| eighth note | quaver | 1/2 | a half-beat rest |
| sixteenth note | semiquaver | 1/4 | a quarter-beat rest |

A **rest** is silence with the same length as the note of that type. Silence is as much a part of rhythm as sound.

</Step>

## Dots, ties and stretching a note

- A **dot** after a note adds half of that note's own length. A dotted half note lasts 2 + 1 = 3 beats. A dotted quarter note lasts 1 + 1/2 = 1.5 beats.
- A **tie** is a curved line that joins two notes of the same pitch. It gives one sound with the length of both added together. It is used across a barline or across a beat.
- A **triplet** divides one beat into three equal parts (instead of two). It gives blues and jazz their "swing" or shuffle feel.

**Syncopation** means stressing a beat that is normally weak, or not stressing a beat that is normally strong. For example, you play a note on the "&" and hold it across the next beat. The ear feels that the sound comes "before" or "after" the pulse. This is the source of most of the groove in pop and funk.

</Step>

## Simple and compound, and where 6/8 comes from

Time signatures come in two families.

| Family | Divides the beat into | Examples | How to count |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | two parts | 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 | 1 & 2 & 3 & |
| **Compound** | three parts | 6/8, 9/8, 12/8 | 1 la li 2 la li |

6/8 has six eighth notes per measure, but the pulse you feel is two dotted quarter notes (three eighth notes each). So it is "two beats, each divided into three". 3/4 has three quarter notes, so it is "three beats, each divided into two". Both add up to six eighth notes, but the weight falls in different places. 12/8 is the slow blues feel that you hear in older songs.

**On the guitar**: the strumming patterns in the guitar path, in the "[Rhythm and Strumming](/en/guitar/rhythm/)" lesson, use eighth notes in 4/4 (right hand down on the numbers, up on the "&"). In 6/8, cells 1 and 4 of the six cells are the two main beats. Strum down on these two points, and the rest are the subdivisions of each group.

<Callout type="note" title="Why not read rhythm from the sound alone">
The ear catches the "pulse" of a song by itself. But writing a time signature lets you tell other people how to play it in groups. It also lets you decide whether a song you hear is 4/4 or 12/8, which sound very close to each other.
</Callout>

**Try it yourself**: play a favorite song, tap your foot to the beat, and count how many beats pass before the strong beat comes back.

</Step>
