## What minor is, and why it has several forms

A minor key has one center note, like a major key. The difference is the third degree, which is a **minor 3rd** (one half step narrower than in major), so the sound is darker. The problem is that degrees 6 and 7 in minor can be adjusted, and Western music uses several forms to solve the problems this causes.

| Name | Formula compared to major | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural minor** (Aeolian) | 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 | The main scale. You get it by starting from degree 6 of a major scale. |
| **Harmonic minor** | 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 **7** | Raises degree 7 to make a leading tone. This makes V major and pulls back to i strongly. |
| **Melodic minor** (ascending) | 1 2 ♭3 4 5 **6 7** | Raises both 6 and 7 so the melody goes up smoothly, with no wide gap. |

**Why harmonic minor exists.** In natural minor, degree 7 (♭7) is a whole step from the tonic. It does not pull back as strongly as in major, where it is a half step away. So composers raise it by a half step. This makes the V chord major (for example E or E7 in A minor, not Em). The side effect is that the distance between degrees 6 and 7 is one and a half steps (an augmented 2nd), which has its own flavor.

**Why melodic minor exists.** Harmonic minor has that wide gap, so a melody going up also raises degree 6. By tradition, in classical music the descent goes back to natural minor. Jazz uses the ascending form in both directions.

## Natural minor and relative keys

A natural minor is A B C D E F G. These are the same notes as C major, starting from A. This is why the key of A minor has no sharps or flats, and why A minor is called the **relative minor** of C major (and C is the relative major of A minor).

There is another term you must keep apart: **parallel minor**. This is the minor key with the same tonic (C minor is the parallel minor of C major). It uses different notes (C minor has E♭ A♭ B♭). The parallel relationship matters for "borrowing chords" ([lesson 7](/en/music-theory/beyond-diatonic/#borrowing-chords-from-the-parallel-minor-modal-interchange)).

| Pair | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| relative | C major and A minor | The same set of notes, a different tonic. |
| parallel | C major and C minor | The same tonic, a different set of notes (degrees 3, 6 and 7 are lowered). |

</Step>

## Pentatonic and blues are subsets

The guitar path, in the lesson "[Scales on the Fretboard](/en/guitar/scales/)", already uses pentatonic and blues. Now let us see where they come from.

- **Minor pentatonic** = natural minor without degrees 2 and 6 (what is left is 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7). One reason is that these two degrees often clash with chords. When you remove them, the set that is left is easy to play over most chords.
- **Major pentatonic** = the major scale without degrees 4 and 7 (what is left is 1 2 3 5 6). This removes the notes that clash with a major chord.
- **Blues scale** = minor pentatonic with a ♭5 added (the blue note).

A minor pentatonic is a subset of A natural minor, and C major pentatonic is a subset of C major. The two seven-note scales use the same notes, so the two pentatonic scales are also the same set of notes, as you see in the guitar path.

## Modes: seven colors from one set of notes

A **mode** means starting the same scale from another degree. The major scale gives seven modes. Each mode has its own sound, because when the tonic moves, the distances from the tonic change.

| Mode | Starts from degree | Compared to | Characteristic note | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionian | 1 | = major | – | Bright, stable. |
| Dorian | 2 | minor with **♮6** | degree 6 raised | A minor that sounds brighter. Cool. Popular in funk and jazz. |
| Phrygian | 3 | minor with **♭2** | degree 2 lowered | Dark, heavy, a Spanish flavor. |
| Lydian | 4 | major with **♯4** | degree 4 raised | Bright and floating, like a dream. |
| Mixolydian | 5 | major with **♭7** | degree 7 lowered | Major in a rock or blues way. |
| Aeolian | 6 | = natural minor | – | Sad. |
| Locrian | 7 | minor with ♭2 and **♭5** | degree 5 lowered | Unstable. Rarely used. |

**A practical way to learn modes.** Do not memorize "Dorian is major starting from degree two", because you have to think backward. Remember **where** a mode differs from major or minor. For example, D Dorian = D natural minor (D E F G A B♭ C) with B♭ changed to B (D E F G A B C). You build it by comparing modes on the same tonic (parallel), which is much faster.

## When modes are used

A mode is not a "special scale that you must play over a chord". It is the name of the sound you get when a song **stays on one tonic long enough that the ear feels it is home**. Here are examples of how a mode appears.

- Looping the chords **Am – G** (i – ♭VII) in A: the ear sits on A as home, and G fits naturally. This is A Aeolian or Dorian, depending on degree 6 (F or F♯).
- Looping the chords **D – C** (I – ♭VII) in D: the ear sits on D as a major home, but there is a natural C. This is D Mixolydian.
- Looping the chords **F – G** (I – II) in F: the major II chord (G) brings in ♯4 (B natural). This is F Lydian.

**On the guitar.** In the guitar path, the lesson "[Improvisation](/en/guitar/improvisation/)" uses pentatonic to stay safe. Modes are the next step, when you want to know how to add degree 6 or 7 without clashing with the chord.

<Callout type="note" title="Do not think of modes as seven finger shapes">
The seven positions on the neck are the same major scale, starting from seven different notes. They become different modes only if the tonic moves too. Playing the same shape over one chord does not turn it into a mode by itself. Your ear must feel where the tonic is.
</Callout>
