## A chord is notes stacked in thirds

A **chord** is three or more notes played together. The most basic chord is built by taking one note (the root) and then **stacking notes every second step of the scale** (skipping one note each time). That means degrees 1, 3 and 5, which is called a **triad**. You can keep stacking to get 7, 9, 11 and 13.

In C major, stacking from C gives C E G. Stacking from D gives D F A. Stacking from G gives G B D. Each one looks different because the **distances** between the stacked notes are not equal (sometimes a major 3rd is stacked, sometimes a minor 3rd).

</Step>

## The four kinds of triad

Each triad is made by stacking two thirds. The kinds differ because they use M3 or m3.

| Kind | Stacked from the root | Intervals from the root | Example from C | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Major** | M3 + m3 | 1 3 5 (M3, P5) | C E G | Bright, stable. |
| **Minor** | m3 + M3 | 1 ♭3 5 (m3, P5) | C E♭ G | Dark, sad. |
| **Diminished** | m3 + m3 | 1 ♭3 ♭5 (m3, dim5) | C E♭ G♭ | Tense, unstable. |
| **Augmented** | M3 + M3 | 1 3 ♯5 (M3, aug5) | C E G♯ | Floating, unresolved. |

Major and minor differ only in the third. The fifth is a perfect 5th in both. So when you learn one shape, you can move the middle note by a half step to get the other chord. This matches what you see when E becomes Em in the guitar path.

**Suspended (sus)** replaces the third with another note. It is sus2 (1 2 5) or sus4 (1 4 5). It is not clear whether the chord is major or minor, so the sound waits for an answer.

</Step>

## Seventh chords

When you stack the seventh degree too, you get a chord with four notes. There are five kinds you need to know.

| Symbol | Name | Formula (compared to major) | Notes from C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 or CΔ7 | major seventh | 1 3 5 7 | C E G B |
| C7 | dominant seventh | 1 3 5 ♭7 | C E G B♭ |
| Cm7 | minor seventh | 1 ♭3 5 ♭7 | C E♭ G B♭ |
| Cm7♭5 or Cø7 | half-diminished | 1 ♭3 ♭5 ♭7 | C E♭ G♭ B♭ |
| Cdim7 or C°7 | fully diminished | 1 ♭3 ♭5 𝄫7 | C E♭ G♭ B𝄫 |

**The name "dominant seventh" is used because** this chord appears on degree 5 of a major key (for example G7 in the key of C), and it is the most tense chord in the key. Its third (B) and its ♭7 (F) are a tritone apart. This is an unstable interval, and it wants to relax to C and E ([lesson 6](/en/music-theory/diatonic-harmony/#chord-functions)).

Build chords yourself. Choose a root and a kind, look at the spelled notes, and listen.

Notice that Cdim7 has B𝄫 (B double flat), not A. The seventh must use the letter B (we stack every second letter), and the diminished seventh is 9 half steps above C, so it must be B𝄫.

## Reading chord symbols

A chord name has a root + a kind + extras.

| Written | Read as | Means |
|---|---|---|
| C | C major | C E G |
| Cm or C– | C minor | C E♭ G |
| C7 | C dominant seven | C E G B♭ |
| Cmaj7 or CΔ | C major seven | C E G B |
| Cm7 | C minor seven | C E♭ G B♭ |
| Cdim or C° | C diminished | C E♭ G♭ |
| Caug or C+ | C augmented | C E G♯ |
| Csus4 | C suspended four | C F G |
| Cadd9 | C add nine | C E G D (no 7) |
| C/E | C over bass E | C E G with E as the lowest note (a slash chord) |

**add9 and 9 are not the same.** Cadd9 adds D to the triad and has no B♭. C9 has both B♭ and D (C E G B♭ D). You must keep them apart, because they sound very different.

**On the guitar.** Every open chord in the guitar path, in the lesson "[Open Chords and Chord Changes](/en/guitar/chords/)", is made from this formula. For example, Am (x 0 2 2 1 0) has the open A on string 5, E at fret 2 on string 4, A at fret 2 on string 3, C at fret 1 on string 2 and the open E on string 1. That gives three notes, A C E, where A and E repeat at different octaves.

</Step>

## Inversions

The same chord can be played in several shapes, by choosing which note is the lowest.

| Position | Lowest note | C major | Written |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root position | the root | C E G | C |
| 1st inversion | the third | E G C | C/E |
| 2nd inversion | the fifth | G C E | C/G |

The chord name does not change. It is still C major, because the set of notes is the same. But the bass changes, so the sound is different. A bass line that moves smoothly (for example C → C/B → Am) is a technique that folk and pop songs use all the time.

**Knowing inversions lets you**

- read a slash chord: the note after the / sign is the lowest bass note.
- understand that the "dozen or so chord shapes on the neck" you play on the guitar are only a few chords arranged in new ways.
