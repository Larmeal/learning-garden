## Music theory is the names for what your ear already knows

People who have played for years without lessons can usually hear the difference between a bright sound and a sad one. They know which chord wants to move on and which melody has finished. All of these things have names, formulas and reasons. Music theory does not make rules that force sounds to behave. It collects what happens again and again in thousands of songs and turns it into a shared language.

You get three benefits right away: you can talk with other musicians, you can move a song to any key, and you can tell why something that sounds good sounds good. This path does not require that you can read music already. It is written to be used together with the guitar path, and each lesson has an "On the guitar" part that shows where the idea lives on the neck.

</Step>

## Pitch is frequency

A sound we call high or low comes from how fast something vibrates. The unit is the hertz (Hz), the number of cycles per second. The reference note A that is used to tune an instrument has a frequency of 440 Hz (A4). This is an international agreement that is used almost everywhere.

| Note | Frequency (Hz), approximate | Remark |
|---|---|---|
| A3 | 220 | Half of A4 |
| **A4** | **440** | The reference note for tuning |
| A5 | 880 | Twice A4 |
| C4 (middle C) | 261.63 | The C in the middle of the piano |

An **octave** is the distance where the frequency becomes exactly twice as high. The ear hears it as "the same note, but higher". This is why two notes one octave apart have the same name.

Western music divides one octave into **12 equal parts**. As a frequency ratio, each step multiplies the frequency by 2 to the power of 1/12, which is about 1.0595. This smallest step is the **semitone** (also called a half step), and it is the distance of one fret on the guitar. (A guitar has its frets placed at positions calculated from this ratio. That is why the frets get closer and closer together in the neck map.)

</Step>

## Sharps, flats and notes with two names

A black key has two names, depending on whether you look at it from the note on its left or the note on its right.

- **♯ (sharp)** raises a note by a semitone. C♯ is a semitone higher than C.
- **♭ (flat)** lowers a note by a semitone. D♭ is a semitone lower than D.
- **♮ (natural)** cancels a sharp or a flat.

C♯ and D♭ are the same sound on the piano and the guitar. This is called **enharmonic**. The correct name depends on the key you are in. For example, the key of D major has F♯ and C♯, not G♭ and D♭, because a scale should use every letter once (D E F♯ G A B C♯, not D E G♭ G A B D♭, where G appears twice and F is missing). You will see the reason in the lesson on scales.

The twelve notes in order, each a semitone apart (chromatic), written with sharps: **C C♯ D D♯ E F F♯ G G♯ A A♯ B**, and then back to C.

## Reading the five-line staff, well enough to get by

Sheet music is written on a five-line **staff**. The height of a note shows its pitch, and its shape shows the rhythm (next lesson). The **clef** tells you which line is which note.

The guitar uses the **treble clef** (also called the G clef).

- The five lines from bottom to top are **E G B D F** (remember it as Every Good Boy Does Fine).
- The four spaces from bottom to top are **F A C E** (they spell the word FACE).
- Middle C sits below the bottom line, on a small extra line called a ledger line.

Two things a guitarist should know:

1. **The guitar sounds one octave lower than it is written.** A note written as E on the bottom line sounds as E3, not E4. If you read middle C in guitar music, the real sound is C3. This is called a transposing instrument.
2. **Tab (tablature)** is a different system. It tells you which string and which fret to play. It is easy to read for guitar, but it does not show detailed rhythm and does not tell you which note it is. It works best together with standard notation.

You do not need to read music fluently to follow this path. It is enough if you can look at one note on the staff and name it slowly.

## Practice before the next lesson

1. Say the twelve notes up and down without looking, in under 10 seconds.
2. Answer at once: what is a semitone above E? (F) What is a semitone above B? (C) These are the two cases with no black key in between.
3. Answer: what is the difference between G♯ and A♭? (The same sound. The name is chosen by the key.)
4. On the guitar, point to every place where you find the note A in the first 12 frets (open A on string 5, fret 5 on string 6, fret 7 on string 4, and so on).
