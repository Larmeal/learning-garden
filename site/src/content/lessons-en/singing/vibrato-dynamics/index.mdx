## Where vibrato comes from

Vibrato is a fluctuation of pitch. Davids and LaTour name four values used to describe it: the rate, how many times per second; the extent, how far the pitch swings from the center note; jitter, the irregularity of the rate; and shimmer, the irregularity of the loudness. A good vibrato in most classical singing has a moderate extent and little jitter, that is, it is regular (chapter 7, page 135).

Vibrato is not produced by an order to do it. It comes from nerve impulses in the larynx at a frequency of about 5 to 8 times per second. When the larynx is relaxed enough, the muscles of the larynx vibrate in that rhythm by themselves. The cricothyroid muscle is the most involved in changing pitch, but the thyroarytenoid is involved too (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 135). Titze proposes that vibrato is a physiological tremor that is stabilized by a mechanism. In an experiment that stimulated the CT muscle of a trained amateur singer electrically at 2 to 8 times per second, the strongest response was found at about 5 Hz, and that person's natural vibrato averaged 5.6 Hz, close to that value (Titze, page 322). This number comes from a single singer, so use it as an example of the idea and not as a typical value for everyone.

Because vibrato comes from natural relaxation, most singers develop it by themselves when they have good support and relax the vocal instrument. Even an untrained singer may have vibrato on long, relaxed notes (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 135). If the voice has too much air or the vocal folds close too firmly, vibrato does not occur much. The best level of closure is 3 to 5 on the scale of one to seven, and level 4 gives the greatest potential (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 140).

The numbers that the book reports are as follows. Most of the rates found are between 5 and 8 times per second and hardly depend on the style, because they depend on physiology. Miller finds 6 to 8 pleasing. Above 8 it sounds like a shake, and below 6 it begins to be a wobble that is heard as alternating notes. Research with both groups of listeners found that 6 Hz was judged the most suitable. Women singers have a slightly faster vibrato than men, and the rate slows with age (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 136).

The extent according to the book is about 30 to 200 cents, counting both the upper and lower parts. A semitone is 100 cents, a quarter of a whole tone above and below the center note. Experts recommend about 100 cents. If it is as wide as 200 cents, the book considers it too much, even in opera (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 136). The reason is the fusion of pitch. When the extent is no more than about 100 cents, the listener does not hear the extremes of the swing, hears only the center note, and perceives the vibrato as fullness of the voice. When it is wider than that, more listeners begin to hear it as a swing, and some do not like it. The book cites data that individuals differ by 50 to 100 times in how well they pick out vibrato, so people who work with voices hear vibrato more easily than other people, which explains why some conductors ask for "straight tone" (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 136).

<Callout type="note" title="There is no true straight tone">
At the physiological level there is always a change in pitch or amplitude from nerve impulses, so there is no "straight voice," only a very small vibrato. "Straight tone" is what the listener perceives (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 135). The "almost none" sample in the lab is therefore set at 20 cents, not zero.

## Vibrato by style

Table 7.1 of Davids and LaTour sums up the use in different styles, stressing that in contemporary styles it is always an artistic choice (chapter 7, page 139).

| Style | Use of vibrato |
|---|---|
| Most classical | Regular, with a moderate rate and extent. A soloist may widen it to emphasize a word. |
| Solo opera | Regular, slower, and especially wider. |
| Legit musical theatre | Regular, a moderate rate, sometimes narrower than classical, slower and wider on emphasized notes or at the end of a phrase. |
| Belt musical theatre | Hardly heard until the end of a phrase, which has a terminal vibrato. |
| Rock | Used little. A ballad may have it to stress emotion. |
| Pop | Sometimes, usually wider on the last note of a phrase. |
| Jazz | Sometimes, narrower than classical and wider on long notes. |
| Gospel | Often used in slow songs and on long notes. If not used throughout, there is usually a terminal vibrato. |

A terminal vibrato is a note that begins with an almost straight sound, and the vibrato comes in the middle of the note and gets wider as the note gets longer, usually together with a louder sound, so the last note "blooms." Try the terminal option in the lab to see it.

The book concludes that in most classical music and choral singing, a moderate vibrato is best for both the listener and the singer. There are three reasons. The listener perceives a better tone color. It helps keep the pitch steady, because the muscles that control pitch have an abnormal vibration and vibrato makes it a more regular rhythm. And suppressing vibrato is tiring, because vibrato is an alternation of contracting and relaxing the muscles (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 136).

## When vibrato is a problem

If you sing regularly and still have no vibrato, common causes are a breathy or hard onset, too much or too little air pressure, insufficient airflow, tension in the vocal instrument, a larynx that is too high, or imitating a singer who hardly uses vibrato. The most common problem is vocal folds that do not close enough or close too firmly. Start at level 4, and do not force the vibrato by shaking the air, the jaw or the neck, because you get an uneven vibrato and waste effort (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 140).

A wobble is a vibrato that is slow (below 5 to 6) and wide but uneven. It usually comes from insufficient breath support (weak abdominal muscles), vocal folds that are too slack, or laryngeal muscles that are not being used, which is common in older singers and in people who sing only at a weekly band rehearsal. The remedies are exercises for a coordinated onset, slides of a fifth so that the breath support is steady, abdominal muscle exercises, the hissing [s] exercise, and practicing at least about 10 minutes a day that includes SOVT (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 140).

A tremolo is a vibrato that is too fast (8 to 10 times per second), usually from tension in the larynx and too much air pressure, and common in younger singers who try to produce vibrato themselves. The remedies are relaxed breathing, onset exercises to get rid of the glottal attack, and exercises to reduce tension (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 140).

Trained singers tend to adjust better. A follow-up study found that voice training reduces the vibrato rate in people who were too fast, raises it in people who were too slow, and makes it more regular (Davids and LaTour, chapter 7, page 140). Warming up before singing also brings the rate closer to the normal range.

## Legato

The word legato comes from an Italian word meaning "tied." A singer ties all the words in a phrase into one continuous sound. It is the main way of singing in classical singing. In true classical style, the resonance, the air pressure and the energy must stay even however the note or the vowel changes, while in other styles legato is more flexible, following the lyric (Davids and LaTour, chapter 11, page 199).

Good legato needs quick consonants and "melting" the final consonant of one word into the next, for example "mist on the moor" is sung as "mi ston the moor." The picture that the book uses is that the vowels are a river that flows continuously, and the consonants are stones dropped into the water as quickly as possible without blocking the current, keeping or increasing the air pressure on unvoiced consonants (Davids and LaTour, chapter 11, page 199). In some languages and for some words you have to interrupt the flow, for example separating "his eyes" so that it is not heard as "hi-seyes."

## Staccato, accent and melisma

Staccato is a quick release of the sound after a coordinated onset, done by stopping the air. Starting with a hit and then closing the vocal folds firmly does not help. As Sundberg explains, the pressure must fall to zero between notes, and then the right pressure for the next note must be built immediately, so it is hard and needs good air control. A helper is to breathe in just a little after each note, and the diaphragm and abdominal muscles will "pulse" slightly when you sing staccato continuously. You must not let the ribs and abdomen collapse (Davids and LaTour, chapter 11, page 202).

Accent concerns the start. If you want to emphasize, increase the air pressure at the beginning of the note and then reduce it at once, so that it feels like the abdomen bounces slightly, instead of using a glottal onset. You get a sound that is loud at the start and then gradually softer (Davids and LaTour, chapter 11, page 203).

Melisma, or a riff, is a passage with several quick note changes per syllable, which many people fear. You can sing it in the classical way by relaxing every muscle that presses on the larynx, plus a gentle pulsing of the upper abdomen. Miller compares it to quiet, fast panting, and a singer whose larynx moves a lot when singing a melisma usually lacks support or tries too hard to control the laryngeal muscles directly. The way to learn is to sing it slowly first to build muscle memory, and then speed up a short section at a time. The "ha ha ha" way can be used as a temporary help, but should not be used continuously because it makes noise, reduces the resonance of the vowels, and makes repeated impacts on the vocal folds (Davids and LaTour, chapter 11, page 203). Not everyone can sing melisma easily. It is like sports, where aptitudes differ and still differ even after a lot of practice.

## Controlling loudness

A singer who uses flow phonation and has a balanced resonance can sing reasonably loud, as can a belt singer, but singing softly while still having a supported sound is harder, especially on high notes. Research found that what gives trained choir singers a wider dynamic range than beginners is that they sing softly better (Davids and LaTour, chapter 11, page 206).

Messa di voce is a popular exercise. Start in the middle of the range with a vowel such as [o], sing very softly, gradually get louder up to forte, and then reduce back to very soft. It is hardest on high notes. The exercise trains control of the air, because you can clearly hear when the start is hit or has air, and it forces you to coordinate registers, from a light sound when soft to a heavier sound when loud. Titze explains it from the point of view of physics: when the sound gets louder, the amplitude of vibration increases, and if you do not adjust the closure of the vocal folds along with it, the sound is pressed in the loud part and breathy in the soft part, so the air pressure and the firmness of closure must change together (Titze, page 301).

When singing softly on a high note, be careful not to release too much support, because this tenses the larynx and squeezes the sound. Henderson (quoted in the book) says to keep the energy in the soft voice alive and supported, and not to use the method of relaxing the arytenoids to open the glottis slightly, because that makes the sound airy (Davids and LaTour, chapter 11, page 206).

<Callout type="warn" title="Special effects: scream, yell, growl">
Shouting, screaming and growling use high air pressure and high closing force of the vocal folds, often begin with a glottal onset and get very loud. If done often or the wrong way, they risk both acute vocal fold injury (for example bleeding) and chronic disease such as polyps and granulomas. Many well-known singers have had surgery or stopped singing for a long time. The book recommends emphasizing breath support in the abdomen, starting with a coordinated or a light glottal onset, keeping good posture, relaxing muscles that are not needed, moving the tongue forward and opening the front of the mouth instead of adding air pressure for loudness, and letting the microphone do some of the work (Davids and LaTour, chapter 11, page 208).
</Callout>
