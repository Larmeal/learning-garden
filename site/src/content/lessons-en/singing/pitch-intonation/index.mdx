## Voice types and range

Voice classification, such as soprano and mezzo-soprano, matters in classical music and in choirs, and matters less in jazz, pop and rock. The range you can sing is one indicator, but there is also tone color, where the voice changes register, how heavy or light the voice is, and tessitura, the range of notes that you sing most comfortably (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 177).

Among adults who have never trained, most men are baritones, with fewer true basses and tenors. Most women are sopranos or mezzo-sopranos, and contraltos are rare. In a choir, an amateur tenor section often has high baritones who use falsetto on the high notes, and the bass section has baritones who do not sing the low notes loudly (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 177).

The book warns about this. A true baritone should be in the bass section of a choir. Singing the tenor part, which is high for that voice, forces you to press the voice until you lose it in the long run. In the same way a woman who is a soprano should not be made to sing alto all the time just because she reads music well or sings harmony well, because the high voice will not be trained (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 177). Many people who sing alto or bass may really be sopranos or tenors who cannot yet reach head voice, because reaching it takes practice and energy. Someone with a big voice who finds head voice hard to enter may be comfortable in the lower part and have the potential to go higher.

<Callout type="note" title="Contemporary singing does not always divide this way">
In contemporary music there is seldom a formal classification of voice types. A musical theatre performer may be cast on being able to mix belt up to a certain note or sing legit up to a certain note, and a pop singer adjusts the key of the song to fit the voice (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 177).
</Callout>

## Range and tessitura

Tessitura is the average pitch of the notes in a song, or the range where most of the notes lie. A song with a high tessitura is hard for a singer, because high notes need more air pressure and more tension in the larynx. When you have to sing outside your comfortable range for a long time, tension builds in the larynx (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 180). A simple guide from the book: if many of the notes are near or above the second transition of women, or the first transition of men, consider the song high in tessitura, and then you need extra places to breathe and should not rehearse only that section for a long time.

A tessitura that is too low can be a problem too. In musical theatre, the soprano parts often have a lower tessitura than in classical, and need a strong chest or chestmix, which a classical soprano may not yet have (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 180).

## Singing higher notes safely

Most people want to use the top of the voice more, and crossing the register transitions smoothly is the key. There are four ways, which we saw in the lesson [Registers and transitions](/en/singing/registers/#four-ways-to-cross-a-transition-smoothly): adjust the vowel and the shape of the vocal tract, reduce the air pressure as you enter the transition, reduce the firmness of closure of the vocal folds, and control the height of the larynx so that it does not rise too far, because if the larynx goes up, the CT cannot work (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 182).

The book adds: relax the muscles of the neck, jaw and tongue, because these muscles press on the larynx and undermine the tilting of the thyroid. The only tension that should be there is in the ribs and abdomen that control the air, and keeping appoggio lets the other parts relax a great deal. If you sing with almost no vibrato on every note, you are tense and high notes are hard, and if the vibrato is present on low notes but disappears on high notes, it usually means you are tense on the high notes. SOVT, especially the straw, helps too.

There is a physical reason why SOVT helps high notes. Research by Titze (1999) that the book cites says that the upper part of the vocal fold, which is used in the upper register, is influenced mainly by the pressure above the glottis, while the lower part of the vocal fold depends on the pressure below the glottis. When there is a partial blockage that slows the air above the glottis, the pressure needed to make the folds vibrate also falls, which helps the vibration of the upper part that is needed for mix and head (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 182).

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: a five-note scale up through a straw">
"Sing" a five-note scale up through a straw, starting at A3 for men and A4 for women, and then move up by a semitone each time. After that, the high notes are often easier to sing, or at least clearer and with less air than before. You can use the guide-note player in the [Warm-up](/en/singing/warm-up-practice/) lesson with the Straw exercise (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 182).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn" title="Do not carry a heavy sound up high">
Singing with a heavy sound at the top of the range risks strain of the vocal folds, quick tiring, and possibly nodules or granulomas. Some singers who rely mainly on "raw" belt have lost their voice for a long time or permanently (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 182).
</Callout>

### Men and falsetto

Many men understand falsetto instinctively and can use it as a bridge to head voice, because in falsetto the TA muscle is relaxed, so a man can experience light singing even before learning head voice. Davids and LaTour recommend singing a five-note descending scale from a high note into the transition area, with tenors starting at D5 and basses at C5, on the vowel [o], alternating head and chest as needed, so that the falsetto blends with the normal voice (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 182).

### Women and the low voice

A woman who can reach chest voice has a wider range than the other voices. The way to learn it is to slide down from the mix range across into the lower voice, starting at B4, sliding down a fifth with low air pressure, and then trying an octave down and leaps, starting no higher than E5. A comfortably low larynx helps lower the formants and makes chest voice warm. Use a straw or [v] to help while you practice (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 184).

## Singing across large leaps

A leap of a fourth or more is hard for many people, especially when it crosses a register transition. The method is to prepare the air pressure before you sing the high note, to connect the low note and the high note in your mind and sing them in the same breath. In classical style, sing the low note in the register of the high note if you can, and if there is a crescendo, begin it from the low note, or the high note will be too loud. Sliding from the low note up to the high note helps build the muscle memory and the air pressure. Tucking the chin just a little helps keep the larynx from being pulled up by the effort of reaching for the high note. For a leap down, singers often release the support too much on the low note, which makes it flat and lacking in resonance (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 185).

The book recommends placing a thick elastic band on the abdomen and pulling it as the note goes up, to help you think of the abdominal muscles instead of trying to control the larynx directly (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 185).

</Step>

## Why the pitch goes off

Teachers and conductors often think a singer is out of tune because they do not listen or cannot tell pitches apart. In reality most people are trying to sing in tune but hear their own voice differently from what the listener hears, because of the sound that comes through bone and the direction in which the sound leaves the mouth, toward the front. Saying "you're flat" does not help if you do not say how to fix it (Davids and LaTour, chapter 10, page 189).

Powell divides the problem into two kinds: horizontal, which concerns each person's melodic line, and vertical, which concerns the harmony with the others in the group. The horizontal kind usually comes from imprecise measurement of the distance between notes, especially "random guess" leaps, and from reading the notes rather than remembering them, which goes away by itself as you get to know the song, and from technique, such as tension in the neck, larynx or tongue, too much or too little air pressure, unsuitable resonance, and using the wrong register (Davids and LaTour, chapter 10, page 189).

Some observations that help. First, a descending melodic line often goes flatter than you think. The book recommends "thinking of the descending interval as smaller than the ascending one" and keeping the support and the resonance space on the way down. Second, think of the pitch of the vowel before you sing, and keep a leading consonant in the same space as the same vowel. Third, singing too loudly or too softly both have an effect. A voice that is too loud is forced off pitch, and one that is too soft has air and does not resonate (Davids and LaTour, chapter 10, page 189).

Resonance affects perceived pitch. A singer who lacks high harmonics is seen as singing flat even when the fundamental is right, and a belt singer who reduces the fundamental may be seen as sharp. When you sing with good technique, good resonance and good support, the pitch often becomes more accurate by itself, and singing in "trained mode" is the best protection (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 85, and chapter 10, page 189).

In a group, listen to yourself and to your neighbors so that you can hear them, and do not sing so loudly that you overpower the others. The condition of the room, how the starting note is given, and even the conductor's beating gestures have an effect. Research found that adult singers with little experience follow a pitch sung by a real person better than a piano pitch (Davids and LaTour, chapter 10, page 189).

## The pitch mirror

The tool below listens to the sound from the microphone, finds the pitch about 30 times per second, and draws the pitch line of the last eight seconds. Nothing is recorded or sent anywhere. Use headphones if you want to press the button to hear a reference note, or the microphone will hear that sound too. Choose the mode "Sing on the note," choose a comfortable note, and sing [ɑ] as a long sound of three or four seconds. Look at what percentage of the time the orange line stays in the green band. If you sing a long enough note and it has vibrato, the tool will estimate the rate and the extent of the vibrato for you.

<PitchMirror client:visible lang="en" />

There are several things to try. Try singing with a lot of air (a breathy onset) and see whether the line starts below or above the note. Try singing across leaps of a fourth or a fifth by sliding, and then getting faster until you do not slide, which is the way the book recommends to practice measuring distances. And try the pivot note exercise, singing "di" on the middle note, going up a semitone and back, down a semitone and back, and then expanding to a whole tone, up to a perfect fifth (Davids and LaTour, chapter 10, page 196).

The tool was tested with synthesized sound. The values it gives are within one cent, and the rate of the vibrato is right. The extent may be off by about 10 percent, but it has not yet been tested with real human voices and a real microphone. Do not trust the numbers as a verdict. It is a second voice for you to compare with what your ear hears and with what a teacher tells you.

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: a slide helps you know how far apart the notes are">
Choose two notes that are far apart, such as a fourth or a fifth, and sing a slow slide from the first note to the second. When both notes are right, gradually speed up until you cut out the slide. Do it both up and down (Davids and LaTour, chapter 10, page 196).
</Callout>
