## How much pressure the vocal folds need to start vibrating

Air can start flowing through the vocal folds as soon as there is a small pressure difference, if the gap between the folds is open, but at first the air flows steadily. A rhythmic vibration needs the pressure to reach a certain value first. Titze calls this value the phonation threshold pressure. In the language of nonlinear dynamics, the vocal folds have to cross a turning point (a bifurcation) from a transient response to a self-sustained vibration (Titze, page 111).

The value is lower when the tissue has little viscosity, when the wave on the mucosa travels slowly, when the gap before the start of the sound is narrow, and when the vocal folds are thicker (Titze, page 111). The first two explain why drinking water makes singing easier. The last agrees with the fact that chest voice, in which the vocal folds are thick, usually starts vibrating more easily than head voice, in which they are thin. Experiments with dog larynges and with three volunteers of each sex found that the value rises when the fundamental goes up, and rises when the tissue is dry (Titze, page 111).

For numbers, Titze gives a formula for the phonation threshold pressure that varies with frequency (equation 9.8 on page 252). At the average speaking frequency of each sex, the value is 0.20 kPa, and it rises as the note goes higher. The air pressure that speakers and singers really use lies between 1.5 and 4 times this value. In the work that the book reports, five professional tenors used about 1.5 to 3.5 times, but untrained people who were tested used more than 4 times to get a similar loudness, which Titze interprets as trained singers using less effort in terms of air pressure (Titze, page 252).

Together, these two points agree with what Davids and LaTour say: singing a high note takes more pressure because the vocal folds are tighter, and dryness makes more effort necessary (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 29, and chapter 3, page 38).

## A sharper puff of air means stronger harmonics

The air that passes the vocal folds in one cycle has the shape of a puff. It starts at zero when the vocal folds are closed, rises as they open, falls as they close again, and stays at zero while they are fully closed. The shape of the puff decides which frequency has how much energy. A puff that closes fast and sharply has very strong high harmonics. A smooth puff has almost none. Titze measures this as the spectral slope, which says by how many dB the harmonics one octave higher are weaker. Normal speech has a slope of about 12 dB/octave. A "brassy" sound has a small slope and a "fluty" sound a steep one. A breathy sound has a steep slope, with noise added between the harmonics (Titze, page 131).

He also points out that a puff with a short opening (a small open quotient) gives a strong second harmonic, and a puff that closes with a slant gives a strong third harmonic (Titze, page 131). The table below is what happens in this site's model, which builds a Rosenberg-type puff with two adjustable values: the share of time that it is open and the share of time that it takes to close. All of the values were run with `verify.mts`.

| Closure level (1 to 7) | Open for what % of the cycle | Harmonic 2 below harmonic 1 | Harmonic 4 below harmonic 1 | Slope (dB/octave) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 97 | 15.2 dB | 31.1 dB | −14.0 |
| 2 | 89 | 14.2 dB | 27.7 dB | −13.3 |
| 3 | 74 | 12.0 dB | 22.9 dB | −12.5 |
| 4 | 62 | 8.3 dB | 18.5 dB | −11.1 |
| 5 | 54 | 6.2 dB | 16.0 dB | −9.1 |
| 6 | 46 | 4.6 dB | 14.7 dB | −7.8 |
| 7 | 39 | 3.3 dB | 13.6 dB | −6.8 |

The numbers in the table belong to the model, and they are values that the author of the lesson chose so that the direction matches the book (firmer closure, stronger high harmonics). They were not measured from real people. Level 4, the medium sound, gives a slope of about −11 dB/octave, close to the 12 dB/octave that Titze states for normal voice, but this closeness is just an observation, not a proof that the model is right.

## Registers in the model, and sudden changes

At the same closure level (level 4), the model builds different puffs for chest, head and falsetto, following what Davids and LaTour describe: chest voice has thick vocal folds that touch over a wide area and give a strong second harmonic, while head emphasizes the fundamental (chapter 8, page 158). The results in the model are

| Register | Open for what % of the cycle | Harmonic 2 below harmonic 1 | Slope (dB/octave) |
|---|---|---|---|
| chest | 53 | 6.0 dB | −9.1 |
| head | 71 | 10.7 dB | −12.5 |
| falsetto | 93 | 18.1 dB | −14.1 |

Titze analyzes a register change that is heard as a clear join (for example from modal to falsetto) as a kind of "spectral slope transition": when the closure of the vocal folds changes slightly, the slope of the spectrum changes suddenly, so the sound changes from "brassy" to "fluty." In the work that he cites, listeners told modal from falsetto by a slope difference of about 6 dB/octave (Titze, pages 283 to 289). In the book he proposes that this sudden change can be reduced by letting the air pressure and the closing force of the vocal folds change together, as in a messa di voce. If this is not done, the sound is pressed when loud and breathy when soft (Titze, page 301). This conclusion fits the advice of Davids and LaTour to reduce the air pressure and the firmness when entering a register transition (chapter 8, page 167).

## What this model can do, and what it cannot

The vocal fold model on this site is a puff of air computed from a simple formula and then passed through the filter of the vocal tract. It can answer questions of the kind "if the vocal folds close more firmly, in which direction do the harmonics change?" But it does not simulate the tissue, does not compute the pressure from the lungs, has no natural vibrato from the muscles, and has no feedback from the vocal tract that helps the vocal folds (what Titze and Davids and LaTour call inertive reactance and feedback). This means it explains the direction of trends, not the numbers of any particular singer.

The same is true of the synthesized sound. We checked the sound by its spectrum, to see that the strong harmonics are where they should be, but we did not ask people to listen and judge whether it sounds like a real voice. The sound you hear may therefore sound mechanical, which is normal for synthesized sound.
