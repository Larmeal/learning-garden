## Where the note comes from: thickness and tension

The frequency at which the vocal folds vibrate depends on two things, thickness and tension. A thicker vocal fold vibrates more slowly, because it has more mass per length, and gives a lower sound. A tighter vocal fold vibrates faster than a slack one. An easy comparison is a violin string: the thinnest string gives the highest sound (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 41).

Two groups of muscles decide these two things. The cricothyroid (CT) tilts the thyroid cartilage forward, so the vocal folds are stretched and thinner, and the note goes higher. The thyroarytenoid (TA) is the innermost layer of the vocal fold. When it contracts on low notes it makes the fold thicker and the sound lower, but in some situations, for example when the note goes higher, the contraction of the TA makes the fold stiffer and vibrate faster. Both groups work on every note, but letting the CT play a bigger part is the key that lets you sing high notes easily and efficiently (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 41).

It has also been reported that the structure of the thyroid cartilage can move by different amounts in different people. About 55 percent of women and 66 percent of men have a structure that moves less, which may make high notes harder to sing (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 41). Knowing this helps you not to lose heart when a friend finds it easier.

We cannot command these two muscle groups directly. We control them indirectly, by thinking of the sound, by adjusting the vocal tract, and by keeping the larynx from rising too high. We will see these methods in the lesson [Registers and transitions](/en/singing/registers/).

There are three things worth noticing. First, as the closure gets firmer, the vocal folds stay together longer in each cycle and close faster, so the air that passes is a sharper puff. Second, a sharper puff gives stronger high harmonics. In this lab the second harmonic is only a few dB below the first when the closure is very firm, but much further below when the closure is loose. Third, when the closure is very loose the air is not really cut off but left open, so you hear air mixed into the sound (breathy).

## Firmness of glottal closure

The only group of muscles in the larynx that we can command is the group that opens and closes the gap between the vocal folds (the glottis). The posterior cricoarytenoid opens the gap, while the lateral cricoarytenoid and the interarytenoid close it, and the interarytenoid is needed to close the back of the gap (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 45). This means we have a tone control that we can command: the firmness of closure.

If the closure is firm, the sound is louder and has many harmonics. If it is too firm without training, the sound is shrill and tense, or "pressed." If the closure is loose or leaves a small gap, you get a sound with air and few harmonics. Davids and LaTour use a scale from one to seven, from very loose (breathy), through level 4, which is the classical or legit sound (Sundberg calls it flow phonation), to very firm, which is the heavy belt sound (chapter 3, page 45).

| Level | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Character | very loose, breathy | | airy | classical, legit | mix belt | | heavy belt |

Different styles use different levels. Pop uses from level 3 (airy) to 5 (mix belt) and can move between them within a single song. Jazz uses about levels 3 to 4. Rock is mostly at belt levels, while a ballad may use a medium level. Traditional musical theatre with heavy belt uses levels 6 to 7, the newer style uses about levels 5 to 6, and gospel and soul cover every level (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 45). The level of closure alone does not make a style. Other techniques go with it.

When the note gets higher and louder, more air pressure is needed, and more firmness of closure is needed to receive that pressure. Cottrell therefore proposed that the resistance at the vocal folds (glottal resistance) is part of breath support, because however well you breathe, if the vocal folds do not resist enough, the pressure below them cannot be controlled. The result is a sound with air, or one that breaks in places (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 45). Herbst extended this idea: support is made up of the breathing system, the resistance at the vocal folds, and the resonance that helps the folds vibrate. They work together and should not be seen separately.

A related number is the closed quotient, the share of time in each cycle that the vocal folds are closed. If they are closed for a long time, the vocal tract can reflect the sound well, because the resonance of the vocal tract works best while the glottis is closed. But if the closed quotient is too high, the sound is pressed (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 45).

## Three ways to start a note

How you start the sound affects the tension of the vocal folds through the whole phrase. If you start the wrong way, it is very hard to fix in the middle of a phrase. Davids and LaTour divide onsets into three kinds, and one special kind (chapter 3, page 49).

A coordinated onset means you breathe in, then close the vocal folds at the same time as you breathe out. It is the method recommended for most singing. Even when you want a very firm closure such as mix belt, or an airy sound with air in it, you can use a coordinated onset with a different firmness. A glottal onset means you breathe in, close the vocal folds, and then relax just enough for them to vibrate into sound. A breathy onset means you breathe out while the vocal folds are still open or not completely closed, so air comes out first. The special method is the vocal fry onset or growl onset, used in rock, gospel and jazz for a rough sound (we look at fry in the lesson [Registers and transitions](/en/singing/registers/)).

### The preparation sequence

Davids and LaTour recommend the following order for a coordinated onset (chapter 3, page 49). Think of the note and the vowel you are going to sing. Then shape the space in the mouth and throat to fit that vowel. Open the vocal folds fully, breathe in quietly without any more movement than necessary, wake up the support muscles, and then breathe out and close the vocal folds in the same moment with the firmness you want. When you are skilled, this shrinks to just "Imagine, Inhale, and Sing."

### When a glottal onset is useful, and when it is a problem

A light glottal onset is useful when you want clarity of words, for example "her eyes" is clearer if you begin "eyes" with a soft glottal, and when you want rhythmic precision in a band. A hard glottal onset has several problems. The sound explodes the vocal folds open, causing noise or a wrong pitch before the note arrives. It can make the sound through the whole phrase harsh, and it weakens the first harmonic a great deal, which sopranos in particular depend on for loudness in the high voice. Repeated use bruises the vocal folds like coughing or throat clearing, and in some people it causes lumps on the vocal folds (nodes or polyps) (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 50). A rock singer who wants a rough sound should use vocal fry instead, which is better for the vocal folds.

On the other hand, a breathy onset, which comes from not coordinating the air with the closing of the vocal folds, causes wrong pitch (usually sharp, because the air flows fast), a noisy sound, and the inability to sing long phrases. An adult who sings with a deliberately airy sound can still use a coordinated onset at low firmness, which is normal in pop styles (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 53).

In teenagers, especially girls, the air in the voice comes from development. The interarytenoid muscle cannot yet close the back third of the glottis, which improves by itself with age and practice (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 53).

## How to end a note

The end of a phrase should be intentional too. The easiest way to stop the air without closing the vocal folds is to breathe in, which happens by itself when you need new air for the next phrase. If you end on a consonant, stop the air together with the end of the consonant. Other ways of ending make strange sounds if they are not meant: closing the vocal folds at the end (glottal release), which makes noise and changes the pitch; closing the mouth at the end of a vowel, which changes the vowel and the pitch; and opening the vocal folds and letting the air go at the end of a vowel, which is the opposite of a breathy onset (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 54). In a band, everyone has to end together, which you can do by placing the final consonant in the rest that follows.

</Step>

## SOVT: singing through a narrow path

Semi-occluded vocal tract (SOVT) means singing with the exit partly closed, for example singing through a straw, singing [v] or [z], singing a lip trill or tongue trill, singing [m] [n] [ŋ], or resting a finger lightly in front of the mouth while you sing a vowel. This partial closing creates a resistance that helps the vocal folds vibrate more easily. Research that the book cites (for example by Titze) shows that the back pressure from the straw changes the shape of the vocal folds so that they work more efficiently. Davids and LaTour recommend doing two or three SOVT exercises in every warm-up (chapter 3, page 54).

The benefits that the book reports are many: singing feels more comfortable, especially on high notes; changing register gets easier; less air pressure is needed to start the sound; the sound is louder for the same effort; the sound is clearer; and resonance is better. The effect remains when you return to real singing (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 54). Two mechanisms can be explained: the tube above the vocal folds (the epilarynx) narrows and couples better with the vocal folds, so that energy returns to help them vibrate, and the shape of the gap between the vocal folds changes to the one that starts vibrating at the lowest pressure.

A straw is a good choice because the resistance is steady. The diameter matters more than the length. A small coffee stirrer has an inner diameter of about 2.5 mm and a soda straw about 5 mm. If the small straw is still hard to use, try a 3.5 to 4 mm straw or use two small straws. A length between 10 and 20 cm makes little difference. Hold the straw in your hand so that the lips do not tense, and check that no air leaks out of the nose (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 54).

The order the book recommends is to start with the one that resists the most and move gradually to those that resist less: the small straw, [v] or [z], humming, lip trill or tongue trill, and the vowel [i] or [u]. At the very least, do the straw, a trill and [i] or [u]. If you have no straw, use [v] instead. A few minutes is enough to see an effect (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 54). The kinds where the sound vibrates in a rhythm (trills) also work like a massage for the vocal folds, and there is research showing that they give about twice the effect of the kinds where the air flows continuously.

What you will notice when you compare is that the high notes feel easier when you go back to the normal vowel. You can use the guide-note player in the [Warm-up](/en/singing/warm-up-practice/) lesson.

## The truth about belt

Belt is controversial for its effect on health. The book sums up two facts. First, chest belt (using chest voice high up with a lot of firmness and a high larynx) needs air pressure about 50 percent higher than classical singing and a very firm closure, so there is a lot of impact between the vocal folds, with the risk of polyps, nodules and granulomas. Mix belt, which almost all modern Broadway performers use, blends the action of the TA and CT, and gives a closing force and air pressure only a little higher than classical, and extends the range up to about G5 in women (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 51). Second, every style carries a chance of vocal fold problems. Research found similar rates of vocal fold injury in opera singers, musical theatre singers and contemporary singers, and belt singers who studied singing have better vocal health. The problem comes from singing without knowing how, more than from the style itself (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 51).

</Step>

## Exercises

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: the three kinds of onset">
Sing "Ha, ha, ha, ha," lingering on the h to feel a breathy onset. Then sing "Uh, uh, uh, uh," closing the vocal folds first like a short grunt, to feel a glottal onset. Between the two is "Ah, ah, ah, ah," the coordinated kind. An image that helps is pulling a handkerchief out of a box smoothly, or pulling a cloth out of a magician's hat (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 60).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: from a lot of air to little air">
Sing a note in the middle of your range on [ɑ] with an airy sound with air in it, and then slowly close the vocal folds more firmly until the sound gets clearer. Do it in reverse too. Practice feeling each level on the scale from 1 to 7 (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 60).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: a good first note makes a good phrase">
Sing [e] on a single note three times, deliberately starting with a coordinated onset. The first two times may have air or a hit, but the third should be better. Then follow it at once with a five-note scale up and down. Notice that a well supported sound stays through the whole phrase. Try the vowel [i] too, which is the hardest vowel for a coordinated onset (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 60).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: end the sound with a breath">
Sing a triad up and down on any vowel, and end the last note with the consonant [t], letting the consonant stop the air, then breathe in for the next phrase. Try again, stopping the sound by closing the vocal folds (a hard release) to feel the difference, and then try ending with a breath in, which gives you the air for the next phrase at the same time (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 60).
</Callout>
