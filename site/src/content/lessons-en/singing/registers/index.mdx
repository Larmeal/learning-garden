## What a register is

When you sing from low to high, you have probably noticed that the voice has places where its color changes. Some stretches feel heavy and loud, others light and thin. These stretches are called registers, a word borrowed from the organ, where each set of pipes gives different notes and a different color. The notes within one register have a similar overall color (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 157).

The person who made this clear was Manuel Garcia, a singing teacher of the nineteenth century, who was the first to use a mirror to look at the larynx and saw that the vocal folds vibrate differently on low and high notes (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 157). Modern measurement with an electroglottograph, which uses a noninvasive electrical sensor, also confirms that the vibration really does differ in each part of the voice.

Scholars do not agree on how many registers there are or what to call them. For learning to sing, we start with the two main groups used in most singing: the lower register, or chest voice, where the vocal folds are thick and the TA leads, and the upper register, or head voice, where the vocal folds are thin and the CT leads. After that comes mixed voice, which blends the two, and then the special registers: falsetto, whistle and vocal fry (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 158).

</Step>

## How chest and head differ

On low notes the vocal folds are thick and touch over a wide area. The TA muscle, which lies under the vocal fold, pushes the lower edge of the fold so that it bulges. This kind of vibration makes the second harmonic strong, so the sound is full, and the closed quotient is high, which makes the resonance even better. Women have a narrower true chest voice than men (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 158).

When the note goes higher, as long as the larynx is not pulled up too high, the CT tilts the thyroid cartilage, so the vocal folds become thin and tight. The TA works less and no longer pushes the lower edge of the fold so that it bulges. The vocal folds touch less, over a smaller area, and move up and down less. The result is that the fundamental is strong, so the sound is softer than chest voice on the same note, but it can go higher (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 158). The term "chest voice" comes from the vibrating feeling in the chest when you sing low, and "head voice" from the vibrating feeling in the head when you sing high. The listener does not hear any resonance from the chest or the head. Only the singer feels it, because neither is a space that makes a resonant sound (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 85). The names are therefore just labels that singers are used to.

In men, head voice is not without the TA. The muscle still helps hold the vocal folds, and in women who sing very high the TA helps hold them too. What matters is that the CT leads, stretching and thinning the folds so that you can go high (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 158).

</Step>

## Mix blends the two

Between chest and head there is a range where the vocal folds work in a blend of TA and CT. It is called mixed voice or middle voice. A study with high-speed video of professional women singers supports two kinds of mix: chestmix, where the TA is dominant with help from the CT and the sound is louder, and headmix, where the CT is dominant, the sound can be soft, and you can go a little higher (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 160). The complexity of the spectrum of a mix lies between head, where the fundamental is strong, and chest, where the high harmonics are strong.

The mix range of men is very short, only a few notes, and that of women is very wide. In practice, if the line of the song is heading toward a high note, use the light sound (headmix) from the low notes of this range, and if it is heading toward a low note, use a heavier sound accordingly. Avoiding mix and singing everything in head makes the sound thin on the low notes (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 160).

Classical and contemporary styles choose differently within the area where there is a choice. Classical or legit singers emphasize the CT so that the transition is smooth and the color is even, while many contemporary styles emphasize the TA in the same area. If you sing mainly classical, practice chest and chestmix (especially if you are a woman). If you sing mainly belt, practice head and headmix (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 162).

For children, the book recommends practicing from the top down, because children sing in their speaking range (chest) by instinct and may get "stuck in the lower voice." Use head voice first until it is strong, then bring that sound down into mix (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 161).

## Where the voice changes (passaggio)

The join between registers is called a passaggio (plural passaggi). There the voice may change color suddenly or break, and in the worst case the vibration stops altogether (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 164). The place where the change happens can tell you the voice type even better than the range does, because the range of someone who has not yet trained fully has not reached its full extent.

All of the figures are approximate ranges, and each person can differ. A study of professional sopranos found the first transition anywhere from D♭4 to A4, and the author admits that the table for women comes from their own experience, which differs from other work that reports a smaller difference between voice types (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 164).

For most men, the transition that causes trouble is the second one, from mix into head. A man who has never trained head voice finds that the color changes a lot around that note, and often has to switch to falsetto (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 164). For women the mix range is wide, so there are many choices around the first transition, but if the move from chest into mix does not use chestmix, there will be problems with the change as well.

The reason for the transition is not in the vocal folds alone. The place around E♭4 matters because there the second harmonic can cross the F1 of the vowel, which makes the vibration unstable. This is a matter of resonance, so some people see the acoustic side as the most important part of some passaggi (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 164). We look at this with a lab in the lesson [Vowels and consonants](/en/singing/vowels/#adjusting-the-vowel-when-you-sing-high).

## Four ways to cross a transition smoothly

Davids and LaTour conclude that four things help you cross a transition with the fewest bumps, and, in the classical or legit style, without a sudden change of color (chapter 8, page 167).

The first is to adjust the formants by changing the vowel or the shape of the vocal tract, so that a harmonic does not cross a formant. The second is to change the mode of vibration of the vocal folds, letting the TA and CT change their proportions little by little. The third is to reduce the air pressure as you enter the transition. The fourth is to reduce the firmness of closure of the vocal folds.

The third and the fourth sound against intuition, because high notes need more pressure, but when the note reaches the top of one register, a lower pressure helps you move to the next smoothly. If the pressure is still high or goes up as you enter the transition, the thinning vocal folds can be pushed apart by the air, the sound stops and there is noise (a register break). A study of women who cross from chest into mix well found that the air pressure fell a great deal (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 167).

Controlling the mode of vibration means controlling the height of the larynx. For head and headmix, the larynx should not rise too high, so that the CT can work through the whole range. For chestmix, the larynx is allowed to rise a little more, to delay the entry of the CT and keep a powerful sound. But if the larynx rises too high, the TA is almost fully shortened, the CT can add only a few notes and the effort is very tiring. If the TA relaxes suddenly so that the CT can take over, the vocal folds react to the sudden change of tension, you cannot control the pitch, and the voice breaks. This is the reason why women can belt only up to about G5 (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 167).

Another group of men use a little nasality to cross the transition. Lowering the soft palate slightly increases the back pressure in the vocal tract, just as nasal consonants do in SOVT, which helps the vocal folds vibrate steadily. Most men who sing classical use this method. The research says that it helps men only (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 167).

</Step>

## Special registers

Falsetto is different from head voice, although some people use the two words interchangeably. In a man's head voice the vocal folds touch along their whole length, while in falsetto they touch only in part and more air flows. A man can sing higher in falsetto than in head voice, the sound is softer and thinner, the high harmonics are weak, and the closed quotient is lower than in chest. Men who sing in a choir should keep falsetto ready to use, because it can sing high soft notes that are hard for head voice (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 170).

Professional singers train a reinforced falsetto, in which the vocal folds close more firmly, giving resonance and vibrato. It is used in pop, rock and classical countertenor, and it lets men reach roughly D5 to B5 with a clear and loud sound. The rock singers that the book gives as examples, such as Freddie Mercury and Frankie Valli, use this method (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 170).

Whistle register is a flute-like sound with most of the energy in the fundamental and the second harmonic, in a range of roughly A5 to G6 in women. Pop singers use it as an effect. Some women can begin to use it from roughly D5 to E5. The way into whistle is to reduce the air pressure and relax the closure of the vocal folds. High-speed video of one soprano suggests that the vocal folds may vibrate along their whole length, which contradicts the picture that only the front part vibrates, so it remains a matter to study (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 170).

Vocal fry (pulse register or strohbass) is a crackling sound in which the vocal folds are very slack. It can be a tool for people who close the vocal folds too firmly, because you can only do it by really slackening them (a tense fry does not help here). In rock, gospel and jazz, a relaxed fry with a lot of air is used as a growl, while in classical singing it is used little, except by basses who have to sing very low notes as the composer wrote them (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 170).

## Exercises

The book recommends practicing registers with a straw first, or with [v] if you have no straw, and then moving on to vowels (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 173).

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: siren or ghost sound">
Imitate a siren or a ghost's wail. Slide quickly up into the upper register and back down into the lower register. Not having to care about the notes lets you pass through every register without excitement, and then you can start to notice how each stretch feels. You can use the guide-note player in the [Warm-up](/en/singing/warm-up-practice/) lesson for the Siren exercise (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 173).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: train head voice">
If you can already reach head voice a little, begin with short slides that stay within a third in head or falsetto, then practice coming down into mix with a descending five-note scale and with slides down a fifth and an octave. Someone who is not used to high notes should keep to short stretches until the muscles are stronger (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 173).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: octave slide by semitones">
Start with [ɑ] on a note in the lower register and slide up one octave, then move the starting point up by a semitone each time until the top note goes well into the upper register. Try sliding from high to low too, which is often smoother. As you go up, rest your fingers lightly on the larynx to see that it rises as little as possible for classical, and not too much for contemporary (Davids and LaTour, chapter 8, page 173).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn" title="Be careful about carrying chest up high">
Singing chest voice up high with a lot of firmness can bruise the vocal folds, and the voice breaks when the TA reaches its limit. We look at the numbers of this risk in the lesson [Vocal folds and onset](/en/singing/vocal-folds/#the-truth-about-belt). If you want a heavy and loud sound, practice mix belt, which uses air pressure and closing force closer to classical singing (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 51).
</Callout>
