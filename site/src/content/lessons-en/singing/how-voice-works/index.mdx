## Who this path is written for

It is for anyone who wants to sing better, whether you sing alone, in a band or in a choir, and who also wants to know how their own voice works. We start with the body (breath, vocal folds, vocal tract) and move toward things you can use straight away, such as starting a note, changing register, singing in tune, and a warm-up plan.

The content comes from two books. The first is Vocal Technique by Julia Davids and Stephen LaTour (second edition), which brings together the science of the voice, voice teaching and choir rehearsal technique, and covers both classical and contemporary styles (pop, rock, musical theatre). The second is Principles of Voice Production by Ingo Titze, which explains the physics of the vocal folds and the vocal tract. We put the page of the book in brackets after a sentence that uses it, and the page number is the page where that section starts. If you own the books, you can open them there and keep reading.

<Callout type="note" title="The sounds on this site are not recordings of real people">
Every sound example in the lessons is synthesized in your browser by a model written after the principles in the books (the last page of each lab gives the details). The model lets you see what changes when you adjust something, but it does not sound like a real singer. Use it as a tool for understanding, then take what you understood to your own voice and, if you have one, to a singing teacher.
</Callout>

## The singing voice is a wind instrument

Davids and LaTour compare the voice with every kind of wind instrument. A wind instrument sends pressurized air past a vibrator, the vibrator makes sound waves, and the waves are reinforced in the body of the tube. In a clarinet the air makes the reed vibrate, and the body of the clarinet strengthens some frequencies of that vibration (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 37).

In the voice, three parts work one after the other. The first is power. It comes from the lungs, ribs, diaphragm and abdominal muscles, which make air pressure. The second is the source of the sound, the vocal folds in the larynx, which turn the air into puffs that repeat in a rhythm. The result is a buzz that is still quiet and is not yet speech. The third is the filter, which means the pharynx, mouth, nose and lips. They choose to strengthen some frequencies of the buzz until it becomes the vowels [i] [ɑ] [u] and different tone colors.

In the science of sound this idea is called source-filter. We will see it as a three-layer graph in the lesson [Resonance and Formants](/en/singing/resonance/), and we will use it to explain almost everything on this path. What to remember now is that the three parts run into each other, and that almost every part can be adjusted. A trumpet has a tube of fixed shape, but a singer can change the shape of the tube at any moment (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 67).

## How the vocal folds vibrate

The two vocal folds first come together. When we breathe out, air from the lungs builds pressure under the folds until the pressure pushes them open. Air rushes through that narrow gap very fast, and the pressure at the gap drops (this is the Bernoulli effect). The lower pressure, together with the elastic recoil of the tissue, closes the folds again, and the cycle starts over (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 37).

One opening and closing is one cycle. The number of cycles per second is the frequency of the sound that we hear as a note. When you sing A4, the vocal folds open and close 440 times every second. In the lab of the lesson [Vocal Folds and Onset](/en/singing/vocal-folds/) we will watch these cycles in slow motion.

The theory that explains vibration this way (myoelastic-aerodynamic) is too simple to explain sustained vibration completely. Titze and his colleagues found that there is also a wave traveling through the tissue of the vocal fold itself, and that a region of low pressure briefly forms above the glottis and helps pull the folds shut (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 37). The simple picture is enough for us, but it is good to know that the real picture is more detailed.

The vocal folds are much smaller than most people think. In X-ray images the folds of a soprano are 14.9 mm long on average, of a contralto 16.6 mm, of a tenor 18.4 mm and of a bass 20.9 mm (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 39). Something this small can make a sound that is heard across a room because the vocal tract amplifies it.

<Callout type="tip" title="Drink water before you sing, not while you sing">
When the body is short of water, the mucus that coats the vocal folds gets sticky, vibration becomes harder, more air pressure is needed, and high notes are especially hard to sing (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 38). The water you drink does not run past the vocal folds, so drink regularly through the day and ahead of time before you sing. We come back to this in the lesson [Tension and Vocal Health](/en/singing/tension-health/).
</Callout>

## Harmonics: one sound with many frequencies stacked in it

The vocal folds do not vibrate at only one frequency. When they vibrate at the frequency of the note, for example C3 at 131 Hz, they also make frequencies that are multiples of it: 262, 393, 524, 655 Hz and so on. These are called harmonics. The first is the fundamental, which decides the note. The energy of the higher harmonics drops quickly, so if nothing amplified them the sound would be almost only the fundamental and would sound hollow and bland. The vocal tract therefore has to strengthen some of them so that the sound is full (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 64).

Our brain reads the fundamental and all of its harmonics as one note. The difference in how much weight each harmonic has is what we perceive as tone color (timbre). This is why two singers can sing the same note and sound different.

## We cannot control the vocal folds directly, so how do we control the note?

The muscles that decide how tight and how thin the vocal folds are, the cricothyroid (CT) and the thyroarytenoid (TA), are not under our direct command. The only group of muscles in the larynx that we can command is the group that opens and closes the gap between the vocal folds (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, pages 41 and 45).

What we can really do is think of the sound we want before we sing. Psychology calls this a mental representation, and it is considered important for developing musical ability. McKinney (quoted in Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 41) says that a beautiful sound begins in the singer's mind, and that if you cannot picture the sound, getting a good sound is only an accident. The picture in your mind should contain the note, the vowel, the loudness and the firmness of vocal fold closure that you want. If you do not think of it first, you tend to land under the target or have to slide up to it.

Hearing yourself also does not match what the listener hears, because the sound that reaches us through bone emphasizes low frequencies, and part of the sound is reflected back from the room (Davids and LaTour, chapter 3, page 39). A teacher, a listener you trust, or a tool that gives feedback helps a great deal. On this path there is the [pitch mirror](/en/singing/pitch-intonation/) that uses a microphone, and labs that show the spectrum of a synthesized voice.

Learning to sing is therefore building a connection between what you hear in your head, how it feels in the body (kinesthetic feedback), and the sound that actually comes out. After enough repetition, the body remembers it by itself.

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: imagine, inhale, sing">
Sing "ah" on one comfortable note, but before you start, sing that note and that vowel clearly in your head. Then breathe in quietly, and then sing. Compare it with singing right away without thinking. Davids and LaTour sum up this order in a short phrase, "Imagine, Inhale, and Sing" (chapter 3, page 49).
</Callout>
