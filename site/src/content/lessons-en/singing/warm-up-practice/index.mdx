## Why warm up

A warm-up is not just a ritual before rehearsal. The authors of the book point out that singers who do not warm up tend to tire their voices within the first 20 minutes of rehearsing songs, and that you should not assume that trained singers have always warmed up beforehand, just like athletes before a competition. Warming up is part of vocal hygiene (Davids and LaTour, chapter 16, page 281).

The benefits that the book gathers include: it reduces tension and lets high notes come out easily, because the muscles are more flexible; it increases blood flow to the muscles, so you can sing hard songs longer and recover faster; it reduces noise in the voice and the variation in frequency and amplitude; it increases the amplitude of the singer's formant; it makes vibrato more regular and reduces a rate that is too fast or raises one that is too slow; and SOVT makes singing noticeably easier. In research with choirs, a vocal warm-up alone gave similar results to a warm-up of body and voice together, but moving the body alone gave no result (Davids and LaTour, chapter 16, page 281).

Another important piece of knowledge is that massed practice (repeating many times within one warm-up) and spaced practice (repeating over a period of several days) are both necessary for building muscle memory (Davids and LaTour, chapter 16, page 281). So you do not need to change the exercises too often. Do the same ones for a long time until that technique becomes a habit.

## The recommended order

The order of the exercises is almost as important as the exercises themselves. Just as you cannot start tennis with an all-out serve, you should go from easy to hard. Davids and LaTour propose eight steps (chapter 16, page 283).

The first is to stretch, to release tension and be aware of how your posture is. The second is to check the breathing: take a few deep, low breaths with the mouth and vocal folds open, the abdomen relaxed and the ribs expanding. The third is SOVT, starting with the one that resists the air the most, such as a straw, and moving to the vowels [i] and [u], where the mouth opening is narrowest. You can slip it in among the other steps. The fourth is to connect the air to the voice, using simple exercises in the lower to mix registers, such as a straw, [v], humming, trills, or alternating [i] and [u], and thinking of a coordinated onset. The fifth is vowels and resonance, using the cardinal vowels arranged along the Vowel Spectrum in the middle of the voice. The sixth is to extend the range, using exercises of small to medium size that are quick and continuous, starting upward for the high notes and downward for the low notes. The seventh is to connect and blend the registers with arpeggios, scales of one octave or more, and octave slides, along with the SOVT with continuous airflow. The last is specific techniques, such as consonants, leaps and melisma, that your songs require.

On choosing exercises, the book says that if a concept or technique is hard, the exercise should be as easy as possible so that the attention stays on the technique and not on a complicated melody (Davids and LaTour, chapter 16, page 283).

</Step>

## Play the guide note, and sing along

The player below has ten exercises from the book. Choose an exercise and a voice part, set the speed, and press play. It plays the guide notes of the exercise repeatedly, moving up by a semitone each time (or two semitones for the Siren) and then back down, and it stops before the top of the comfortable range of the chosen part, using the numbers from Table 9.1 (Davids and LaTour, chapter 9, page 177). So you are not led to a note that is too high without knowing it, but if your voice does not match the chosen part, choose the nearest one and shift it yourself by ear.

The list "Warm-up sequence of about 12 to 15 minutes" below the player is Sample sequence one of the book (chapter 16, page 286). For each step that has a matching exercise, press it to load it into the player. For steps without a guide sound, such as stretching or breathing, follow the related lesson, which is [Posture and breath](/en/singing/posture-breath/#exercises).

While you sing, use the [pitch mirror](/en/singing/pitch-intonation/#the-pitch-mirror) to check whether you are on the note, but mute the guide-note player and use headphones if you turn on the microphone, or the microphone will hear the guide note and confuse the detector.

<Callout type="warn" title="Stop if it hurts or feels forced">
Every exercise must not hurt, feel forced, or need tension. If you feel that the voice is tired or dry before the end, reduce the number of rounds, sing softer, or stop. Extend the range slowly, according to your comfort, which differs from day to day.
</Callout>

## Cool-down

After heavy singing, spend a few minutes letting the voice relax. The book warns that there is not much research on cool-downs for singing, but the experience of singers and teachers suggests that it helps, and singers who cool down regularly report that the voice feels good, recovers faster, and returns to normal speaking sooner. Singing softly also reveals hidden tiredness, which you can use to plan the next rehearsal (Davids and LaTour, chapter 16, page 287).

The order that the book recommends is: stretch the shoulders and neck; sing slides up and down through the whole range through a straw (or [v] followed by the vowel [æ] or [ɑ]); do a lip trill or tongue trill in the middle range; and a short descending scale on [u] with a coordinated onset at medium firmness (Davids and LaTour, chapter 16, page 287).

</Step>

## Plan a weekly practice

Now that you know what each lesson does, let us make a simple plan that you can really follow. This plan is a proposal by the author of the lesson and does not come directly from the book. Adjust it to the time you have and to what you want to improve. Use the principle of the book that frequent, short practice is better than a long session once in a while, and do not repeat a high-tessitura passage over and over until you are tired.

| Day | Time | What to do | Review lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every day | 10 to 15 minutes | Warm up in order, then sing a song you like for 5 to 10 minutes, and finish with a short cool-down | [Warm-up and practice plan](/en/singing/warm-up-practice/) |
| Day 1 | Add 5 minutes | Hiss on [s] or [v] with a timer, and check your breathing posture in a mirror | [Posture and breath](/en/singing/posture-breath/) |
| Day 2 | Add 5 minutes | The three kinds of onset, then practice a coordinated onset on the vowel [i] | [Vocal folds and onset](/en/singing/vocal-folds/) |
| Day 3 | Add 5 minutes | Siren or octave slide through your transition, led by the straw | [Registers and transitions](/en/singing/registers/) |
| Day 4 | Add 5 minutes | Cardinal vowels and alternating [i] and [u], checking the sound in the pitch mirror | [Vowels and consonants](/en/singing/vowels/) |
| Day 5 | Add 5 minutes | Sing the hard passage of a favorite song on a single vowel, then add the words | [Range and singing in tune](/en/singing/pitch-intonation/) |
| Day 6 | Add 5 minutes | Messa di voce, and look at your own vibrato in the mirror | [Vibrato and control of the voice](/en/singing/vibrato-dynamics/) |
| Day 7 | Rest or light | Relax the body, drink water, and check whether the voice is tired | [Releasing tension and caring for the voice](/en/singing/tension-health/) |

If you have a singing teacher, use this lesson as a common language for talking, and bring in the exercises your teacher gives in place of the ones in the table. A teacher hears and sees what the tools on this site cannot, and people who learn to sing on their own are often at greater risk of vocal fold problems. The book recommends studying with a teacher in order to learn ways that protect the health of the voice (Davids and LaTour, chapter 15, page 257).

</Step>
