## Where the tip of the tongue should rest

Before going through how to make each vowel, you need to know the resting position of the tongue. Davids and LaTour say that whatever vowel you sing, the tip of the tongue should rest touching the base of the lower front teeth, a little above the gum. The part of the tongue that touches the teeth differs slightly with the vowel, for example [i] touches more (chapter 5, page 93). If the tip of the tongue rests on the gum, the tongue is pulled too far back, the sound is dull, and the tongue is tense, which affects the larynx too, because the tongue and the larynx are connected to the same hyoid bone. And you must not press hard on the teeth, because this is a "resting" position.

In classical singing, after every consonant you must bring the tip of the tongue back to the resting position at once. If you do not, the vowel that follows becomes an unintended neutral vowel, which makes the vowels hard to tell apart, makes the pitch go off, and keeps a choir from blending well. Even contemporary styles should return quickly, unless you deliberately stretch a consonant for the performance (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 93).

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: the tip of the tongue and the vowel">
Rest the tip of the tongue at the base of the lower teeth and sing [ɑ], then try moving the tip of the tongue forward over the teeth, and back down to the gum. The vowel will change. Then try flicking the tip of the tongue up and down while you sing, and you will hear the vowel change in another way. This is what happens to a singer who leaves the tip of the tongue halfway after a consonant (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 93).
</Callout>

</Step>

## The vowel map

Vowels differ in four ways: how high the tongue is, where the highest point of the tongue is, how far the jaw opens, and the shape of the lips. The picture of Davids and LaTour arranges the vowels along a U-shaped curve called the Vowel Spectrum, from [i], where the tongue is high at the front and the mouth is wide and flat, through [e] and [ɑ], where the tongue is low and the jaw is open, to [u], where the tongue is high at the back and the lips are round (chapter 5, page 94). Vowels that are next to each other on this line are made in a similar way, which is easier than remembering each one separately.

The lab below places the vowels on a chart of F1 against F2, in the same arrangement as the Vowel Spectrum: [i] at the upper left, [ɑ] at the lower right, and [u] at the upper right. Press the name of a vowel to listen, or press and drag anywhere to hear the vowels in between.

Try the order that the book recommends. Rest the tip of the tongue at the base of the lower teeth. Sing [i] in the middle of your range with a slight smile. Lower the jaw by about the width of a little finger between the molars and sing [e], letting the highest point of the tongue move back and down. Move the tongue back and lower it again until it is [ɑ], lowering the jaw a little more. Then raise the tongue and round the lips into [o] with the jaw closing slightly, and finally raise the tongue higher, round the lips more and close the jaw until you reach [u]. Try going back and forth between two neighboring vowels on one note, changing smoothly with no break in the resonance (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 94).

What to notice is that the jaw lowers a lot from [i] to [e], and then a little more to [ɑ]. If the jaw does not lower enough at [e], the back of the mouth is too narrow and the [e] is shallow and sharp. On the other hand, if the jaw goes down too far in the middle of the voice, the soft palate usually goes slack and the sound goes flat. A singer who changes from [e] to [ɑ] should pay more attention to the position of the tongue than to lowering the jaw, and should not move the jaw more than needed when changing vowels, because it makes F1 change while you are singing a single vowel (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 94).

</Step>

## Bright and dark

Some vowels sound brighter than others. Brightness depends mainly on F2, with F1 and F3 also involved. Vowels with a high F2 sound brighter. In order from the brightest, they are [i] followed by [ɪ] [e] [ɛ] [æ] [a] [ɑ], and the darkest is [u] followed by [ʊ] [o] [ɔ]. The dark vowels have a low F2 that is very close together. What separates them is F1: [ɔ] has the highest F1 and [u] the lowest, so [ɔ] is the brightest of the dark vowels (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 100).

You can test this yourself. Put a hand beside the ear with the finger pointing up, sing [ɑ] in the middle range, and slide the finger to in front of the nose while raising the tongue higher, and the sound gets brighter. Then slide the finger to the back of the head while pressing the tongue down, and the sound gets darker. For classical singing the goal is a color that is neither too dark nor too bright, which is the position "opposite the ear," while many kinds of belt want a color in front of the ear (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 100).

</Step>

## Vowels in contemporary styles

Contemporary styles always want the listener to hear what is being sung, because a song is a story, and the color is often brighter than in classical, and does not need to stay the same color through the whole range. Pop, country and musical theatre use vowels more like speech, with more resonance in the mouth than in the pharynx, so the back of the jaw is closed more, the mouth opens wider, and the tongue is more forward and higher, for example an [u] with less rounded lips, and an [e] that is brighter if the tongue is higher and more forward. Musical theatre is the brightest, jazz is often softer, and gospel in all forms is usually bright and balanced (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 101).

Teachers of mix belt use front vowels such as [e] and [æ] to help get a bright sound, and starting with w and y (for example "way," "yay") helps you resonate in the mouth more than in the throat. Someone who sings mainly contemporary and wants to practice a classical sound can use [o] and [ɑ] (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 101).

When you sing in a choir, everyone has to sing the same vowel in the same way, or the formants of each person differ and they cannot blend. A review of the research concluded that matching the formants of vowels among singers is one of the most important factors in blending (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 102).

</Step>

## Vowels that are hard to sing

The vowel [u] is often made wrong in three ways: the lips are not round enough so the sound is shallow and has no resonance, not enough space is made in the mouth, and the tongue is brought too far forward for a back vowel. Many teachers correct it by having you round the lips more, but lips that are rounded too much make the opening narrow and pull the cheeks in, so the space inside is reduced and the sound turns into a shrill "hoo." And since [u] already has the lowest F1 and F2, squeezing the lips narrower lowers F1 even more, affecting the sound and the pitch. The way to practice is to change from [o] to [u] smoothly, closing the jaw and rounding the lips a little more while keeping the resonance of [o], and to practice alternating [i] and [u] (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 102).

The vowel [i] sometimes sounds too bright for a choir, so the conductor asks for [ɪ], but this makes it harder to hear and the resonance falls. Before reducing the brightness, check first whether the smile is too wide, whether the back of the jaw is lowered enough, and whether there is nasality (nasality reduces the F1 that is wanted for classical sound). Singing [i] in the mouth space of [u] gives a poor "hoo" sound (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 102).

For the vowel [ɑ] in classical style you do not need to open the mouth wide at the front. What matters is to lower the back of the jaw. Opening too wide often happens when you sing high and loud, and tension often follows. In contemporary styles, opening wide is part of getting a bright sound. What is wrong in every style is tension in the neck and face and pushing the head forward (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 102).

The neutral "uh" vowels, both [ʌ] in the word love and [ə] in the last syllable of pencil, are often dull and lifeless and hard to blend. The remedy is to lean toward [ɑ], for example the word love can be sung with [ɑ] when you sing it, unless you want to emphasize it, and [ə] should be sung somewhere between schwa and a nearby vowel so that it has resonance without standing out too much (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 102).

Another thing to watch is "covering," adjusting the vowel to make it darker so that the sound is even, for example singing [i] in the space of [u], so that the sound is "put under a lid," as Fleming puts it. If you do it too much, or do it in the middle of the voice, the sound is too dark and the words cannot be heard. A test with expert listeners by Carlsson and Sundberg found that in comparisons, 98 percent preferred singing that kept the formants constant over adjusting the formants on every note of a one-octave range (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 102).

</Step>

## Adjusting the vowel when you sing high

Most singers have to adjust the vowel as the note goes higher. The reason is that when a harmonic of the note (including the fundamental) climbs across F1, the vibration of the vocal folds becomes unstable, and the voice may break, change color, or change pitch suddenly. But if the harmonic is a little below the formant, the energy comes back to help the vocal folds and reduces the strain and effort (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 107). Before going into detail, try this lab. It builds five notes of a soprano and compares the sound pressure when the original vowel is left alone with when F1 is raised to sit a little above the note.

For classical women, when the note goes above roughly C5 and enters head voice, F1 has to be raised to sit a little above the fundamental, by lowering the jaw and opening the mouth more as the note gets higher, and smiling slightly on very high notes to make the tube shorter. This moves the vowel a little toward [ɑ], but it is not singing full [ɑ]. For example, lowering the jaw while singing [i] while keeping the tongue gives [ɪ], the next vowel on the way toward [ɑ], and [o] becomes [ɔ] (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 107). You should begin before it is necessary, and not adjust too much on low notes. Adjusting more than one or two positions on the Vowel Spectrum makes the sound seem affected, except for a soprano singing above A5, who has to adjust every vowel to [æ] [a] or [ɑ], because the fundamental is above the F1 of every vowel (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 107).

For classical men, the method is the opposite of what you would think. You have to limit the jaw lowering when singing high, so that F1 stays below the second harmonic and they do not cross, and avoid vowels with a very low F1 such as [i] and [u], which have to be changed to [ɪ] and [ʊ] with the tongue moved forward so that F2 amplifies the third or fourth harmonic. Middle vowels such as [ɪ] [ɛ] [e] [ɔ] [o] [ʊ] work best. Men in contemporary styles do the opposite: they open the mouth into more of a trumpet shape when the note is above roughly C4, to push F1 to amplify the second harmonic, which works up to a point, and then the voice often breaks into falsetto (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 107).

Contemporary women who use chest or chestmix raise F1 with the note by letting the larynx rise slightly, smiling wide and opening the mouth, beginning from the range D4 to D5, which differs from classical singing, where there is hardly any adjustment in this range (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 107).

As for where to begin adjusting, according to the book, for classical style a small adjustment should begin as you enter the first transition: about E4 for soprano and tenor, about D4 for mezzo-soprano and baritone, and about C4 for contralto and bass. Women should begin adjusting F1 toward [ɑ] as they approach the second transition, about E5 for soprano, about D5 for mezzo-soprano and about C5 for contralto, and begin earlier for vowels with a low F1 such as [i] and [u] (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 107).

## Consonants

Consonants matter no less than vowels, but there are two main problems: singers often change the vowel before and after a consonant when they make the consonant slowly (Davids and LaTour call this "vowel pollution"), and they often neglect consonants because they want a beautiful vowel (chapter 6, page 123). In classical singing, consonants should be quick and precise, the tip of the tongue returns to the resting position at once, and the jaw does not move more than necessary. A consonant made slowly, such as [bl] in the word blow, may become "buh-lo," and if a consonant at the start of a phrase is slow, the pitch is often low and you have to slide up. Think of the consonant at the same pitch as the vowel that follows, and breathe in with the vocal tract already in the shape of that vowel and note, even if the word begins with a consonant (Davids and LaTour, chapter 6, page 123).

Consonants that need the jaw or lips to close (b j m p s v z) cause a "slide" in pitch if they are made slowly, because when the jaw lowers, F1 rises and the perceived pitch goes up, and when the jaw closes it goes down. Close and open quickly. The consonants g k ng use the tongue against the palate, which makes the air stumble and the resonance easy to lose, and over-pronouncing [g] makes noise and can make it [k], so that the meaning changes (Davids and LaTour, chapter 6, page 125).

The consonants [w] and "y" are really vowels, called glides. Think of [w] as an [u] with the lips rounded more and then changing quickly to the next vowel, and "y" as an [i] with the tongue closer to the palate (Davids and LaTour, chapter 6, page 127). Consonants come in pairs that use the same organs and differ in whether they are voiced, for example [v] and [f], [g] and [k], [d] and [t], [z] and [s], [b] and [p]. Choosing [z] in place of [s] at the end of a word such as "praise" gives a softer ending, and starting with an unvoiced consonant means the air is already flowing as you enter a high note (Davids and LaTour, chapter 6, page 127).

In contemporary styles, the length of a consonant is an artistic choice. Stretching a consonant helps the words be heard clearly in musical theatre. In a very resonant room you should emphasize consonants more, but in classical you should not overdo it, because too much jaw movement spoils the resonance and the vowels. Final consonants, which are often forgotten, must not be dropped, and you must not add "uh" to the end of a word (Davids and LaTour, chapter 6, pages 124 and 131).

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: change the consonant, not the vowel">
Sing bah, mah, pah, sah, vah quickly on one note, without letting the pitch slide, and with the vowel the same in every one. Then try words with consonant clusters such as blow, clear, glory, combining the initial consonants into one efficient movement (Davids and LaTour, chapter 6, pages 125 and 126).
</Callout>

## Exercises

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: sing only the vowels first">
If there is a song that does not flow well, try singing the lyrics pronouncing only the vowels (writing the IPA above the line can help) until the sound is continuous without a break, and then add the consonants. Add them quickly, like dropping consonants into a river of vowels that keeps flowing (Davids and LaTour, chapter 11, page 199).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: slide up an octave and let the vowel change by itself">
Start from the lower to middle part of your voice and slide up one octave on different vowels, letting the vowel change by itself as the jaw needs to lower, without setting the final position beforehand. Notice how the vowel changes and how much you need to lower the jaw. Check that the air pressure stays the same while the note rises. Classical men should begin with [ɑ] and lean toward [ɔ] as they enter the transition, and gradually move the tongue forward (Davids and LaTour, chapter 5, page 107).
</Callout>

Use the guide-note player in the [Warm-up](/en/singing/warm-up-practice/) lesson to practice five notes up and down on the cardinal vowels and five notes down from [i] to [a].
