## Tension that is needed, and tension that is not

Singing needs some muscle tension, for example the muscles that close the gap between the vocal folds and the abdominal muscles that control the air pressure. The problem is tension that is more than needed or in the wrong place, which spoils the tone color and the health of the voice, both in the short term and in the long term. Davids and LaTour identify the six places that are found to be most troublesome: the neck, the larynx and pharynx, the jaw, the tongue, the lips, the shoulders, and the legs (chapter 14, page 245).

The good posture we talked about in the lesson [Posture and breath](/en/singing/posture-breath/) is the first step toward reducing tension, and there is an observation that applies to every place: tension usually compensates for something missing in technique, such as not enough air, or it is the way the body tries to add power to the voice. If we aim at what needs to happen, such as keeping the support and thinking of the sound we want, the extra tension often falls by itself. And when you relax one place, people often move the tension somewhere else, for example relaxing the hands and then tightening the feet, so you need to check the whole body (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 253).

## Neck, larynx and pharynx

Most of the muscles that control the vocal folds cannot be commanded directly, but many people try to control them directly because they think they can adjust the pitch that way. The result is unnecessary tension. McKinney says that a sound that is pressed, tight and harsh on the ear comes from tension in the vocal folds and in the other muscles of the larynx and neck, and it is worse if the air pressure is also too high (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 245).

The pharyngeal constrictor muscles come in three layers. The upper layer attaches to a ligament at the side of the back jaw, the middle attaches to the hyoid bone from which the larynx hangs, so it affects both the pharynx and the larynx, and the lower attaches directly to the thyroid cartilage. When they tense, the pharynx narrows, which causes problems of pitch, vibrato and a tight sound (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 245). Many contemporary singers squeeze the pharynx to get brightness, but the book says they should not, because there are ways to get brightness without tension: lowering the soft palate slightly, raising and moving the tongue forward, letting the larynx rise a little by itself, and smiling wide (chapter 14, page 245).

Some people tense the neck from a habit of speaking without air support. When the air runs out at the end of a sentence they squeeze the throat to push the air out. If you bring this habit into singing, the result is much worse. Practicing support in singing and in speaking with air helps (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 245).

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: sing with the head tilted back as if drinking from a bottle">
Edwards suggests an exercise that helps you feel what the voice is like when it is not tense. Tilt your head back to look at the ceiling as if you were lifting a bottle of water to drink, which stretches the pharyngeal constrictor muscles so that they find it hard to contract. Sing short patterns on [ɑ] and compare them with when the head is in the normal position, including high notes, because tension is often there. Alternate between the two postures until you can sing without squeezing (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 245).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: relax the neck">
Tilt the head to each side, then bow forward, and then slowly roll it in a half circle toward the back and return. Put the left hand on the right side of the head and pull gently so that it tilts left until you feel the right side of the neck stretch. Do it on both sides (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 254).
</Callout>

</Step>

## The jaw

Tension in the jaw is common, sometimes because of a misunderstanding that you must lower the jaw a lot, or because after lowering it a lot you have to hold it tense, which passes the tension on to the larynx. For classical singing, beginning a yawn by just the first 10 percent is enough to release the back of the jaw, while contemporary styles emphasize lowering the front of the jaw (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 247). Vennard said that "a tight jaw is the symptom of a tight throat." The picture to avoid is a tense neck and face with the head pushed forward while the mouth is open wide. Opening the mouth wide alone may suit belt.

If the jaw hurts, crackles or moves with difficulty, it may be a temporomandibular joint disorder (TMJ). The causes include grinding the teeth in sleep, chewing a lot of gum, poorly aligned teeth, or a poor bite. Cold packs and massaging the joint help, but if it does not improve you should see a dentist who is skilled in TMJ (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 247).

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: shake the jaw and hum while chewing">
Sing a long [ɑ] while gently massaging the jaw joint in front of the ear and the cheek muscle (masseter) used in chewing, or hum while moving the jaw in slow circles like a cow chewing grass while you sing, as the book recommends (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 254).
</Callout>

</Step>

## The tongue

The tongue is a large muscle that is joined to the hyoid bone, from which the larynx hangs. Tension in the tongue therefore easily changes the resonance and the larynx. A tongue that is pulled back or flattened too much pushes the larynx down too far, lowers every formant frequency, and covers the high sound, giving a "throaty" sound. The tongue should not be pressed flat on purpose to make space in the throat, because this makes the resonating chamber shorter and narrower (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 249).

A check that Tetrazzini recommends is to feel under the jaw behind the chin. If the tongue is tense (especially if it is pressed down), you will feel a hard lump. As for the groove in the middle of the tongue when you sing [ɑ] on a high note, experts disagree on whether it is a sign of tension or something that happens by itself when the voice is relaxed. The book considers that a shallow groove develops by itself with practice, and you should not try to create it, because it makes you tense (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 249).

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: relax the tongue">
Put a finger on the larynx, move the tongue back from its resting position, and feel the larynx being pushed down. Sing [ɑ] in the middle range with a relaxed tongue and compare it with when you press the tongue, and you will hear a big difference. If your tongue tends to drop back, try a short scale on [ɑ] with the tip of the tongue sticking out slightly in front of the lips, and then returning to rest at the base of the lower teeth, or use consonants that touch the gum ridge, such as t and d, ahead of a front vowel (tea, deep, tick, day) as a spring that keeps the tongue forward (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 254).
</Callout>

## Lips, shoulders and legs

Tension in the lips includes holding them rigid, a stiff smile all the time, or pressing the lips against the teeth so that the sound is dull, and rounding the lips too much even when you sing [u]. For classical, smile by slightly lifting the upper lip and cheeks. If you can see the four upper teeth, it is not yet too much (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 252).

Raised shoulders always affect the larynx, because they pass tension to the neck, whether held up or raised as you breathe in. Holding a heavy music folder also raises the shoulders: "shoulders are not for holding music." Shoulders hunched forward reduce the space that the lungs can expand into, because they press down on the breastbone and ribs (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 252). Legs that are tense for a long time shake, and locking the knees is very common, which reduces the return of blood to the heart and the brain and is the main cause of fainting among people standing on a choir riser. You do not need to bend the knees too much to compensate (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 253).

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: relax the shoulders">
Raise the arms above the head and stretch toward the ceiling, shrug the shoulders several times, and then roll the shoulders in circles backward and forward, letting them relax down into their sockets. If the shoulders are hunched, try pulling the shoulder blades together and holding for ten seconds. This posture is exaggerated and is used only to correct a hunching habit. It is not the posture you want when you sing (Davids and LaTour, chapter 14, page 254).
</Callout>

</Step>

## Water and what you eat

The truth about drinking water is that when the body is short of water the vocal folds vibrate with more difficulty, more air pressure is needed, the sound has noise in it and the pitch is not stable. The review that the book cites concludes that voice quality gets worse when the body is dehydrated overall and when the surface of the vocal folds is dried out by breathing through the mouth (for example while sleeping), and gets better when you drink enough water and when you inhale steam. If you feel thirsty, you are already short of water. You should drink regularly through the day, and ahead of time rather than while singing, because the water you drink does not run past the vocal folds. After waking up the body is short of water, so drink a couple of glasses if you are going to sing in the morning. Singing softly in a warm shower helps too, and people living in dry air should have a humidifier. Well hydrated vocal folds are harder to injure and recover faster (Davids and LaTour, chapter 15, page 258).

Table 15.1 of the book sums up the effects of drinks and drugs. Alcohol dehydrates you, so drink more water. Coffee and caffeine in people who drink them regularly do not dehydrate, according to the research the book cites. Diuretics dehydrate you and require more air pressure. The antihistamine diphenhydramine (Benadryl) does not dehydrate the vocal folds according to research, but be careful about drowsiness. Antihistamines combined with decongestants may make the mucus stickier (Davids and LaTour, chapter 15, page 258). This is general information and not advice about medication for anyone. Ask a doctor or pharmacist when you want advice specific to you.

For eating before you sing, the body needs energy, but a full stomach makes it hard for the diaphragm to descend and may cause burping or acid reflux. The book gathers recommendations to eat a moderate meal 1.5 to 3 hours before singing (Davids and LaTour, chapter 15, page 262). You should be awake at least two hours before you sing so that the sinuses drain and the mucus thins, and research found no evidence that milk and dairy products increase mucus, although according to one study people with chronic mucus problems may benefit from avoiding dairy.

## Resting the voice, and what you should not force

The voice muscles must be used beyond what they are used to in order to develop, and they need time to rest after heavy use, as in physical exercise. Continuous rehearsal of singing should not exceed two hours, and three hours of rehearsal needs a break of at least 15 minutes. Brancaccio proposed a point system that the book cites: half an hour of choir rehearsal, singing lessons, solo practice, or loud speaking in a noisy place counts as 10 points, and one hour of relaxed speaking in bursts also counts as 10 points. The goal is no more than 100 points a day, and no more than 50 if you are sick or your voice is tired. Talking during a break does not count as resting the voice. You should have "short voice rests" during the day, speak at a suitable pitch, neither too low nor too high, because it is like singing at the end of your range for a long time, use a straw during warm-up and cool-down or when the voice is tired, and when you rehearse a passage with a hard tessitura, use "marking," such as singing it an octave up or down or singing softly (Davids and LaTour, chapter 15, page 260).

Stress and worry also affect the voice. The main job of the larynx is to protect the airway, and under stress the muscles may go back to that job, which makes the voice tight, shaky or broken. However, singing itself reduces stress. Work that the book cites reports that singing, especially in a group, is linked with feeling good, better immunity and higher endorphins (Davids and LaTour, chapter 15, page 261).

<Callout type="warn" title="When to stop singing and see a doctor">
If your voice stays hoarse for a long time, your throat hurts when you sing, the voice goes missing in some places (usually high notes), the voice has a lot of air in it, or it starts slowly, these are signs that the vocal folds may be swollen. Rest your voice. The book suggests a simple test: sing "Happy Birthday" in staccato in head voice or falsetto, and try vocal fry. If the vocal folds are inflamed, it is hard to do. If it is easy, you can continue singing. If not, rest. The most dangerous time is while you are recovering from illness, because tissue heals more slowly than it feels, and rushing back to full singing risks permanent damage such as granulomas and nodules (Davids and LaTour, chapter 15, page 264). The whole lesson is general information and does not diagnose or treat.
</Callout>

Acid reflux can damage the larynx without causing heartburn in the chest. It is called laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR). The symptoms include a slight change in the voice, inability to sing for long, hoarseness, cough, a feeling of a lump in the throat, and an unusually long time needed to warm up, especially in the morning. Prevention is not eating or drinking alcohol within three hours before going to bed, and controlling your weight. If you have symptoms, consult a doctor (Davids and LaTour, chapter 15, page 263). As for when you have a cold, if the soft palate can still close off the nose, a blocked nose does not make the listener hear a difference, even though the singer feels one. But if the throat and tonsils are swollen, the resonance of the pharynx is spoiled because the soft, swollen surface absorbs the sound, and hard coughing or throat clearing hurts the vocal folds. Swallow instead of clearing your throat (Davids and LaTour, chapter 15, page 264).

</Step>
