## One tube closed at one end

If we reduce the vocal tract to the very simplest form, it is a straight tube with the vocal folds closing one end (the gap between the vocal folds is very narrow compared with the tube) and the lips open at the other end. Titze calls this kind of tube a quarter-wave resonator, because at the lowest resonance the length of the tube is exactly one quarter of the wavelength. The resonance frequencies of a tube of length L are F(n) = (2n − 1) × c ÷ 4L, where n is 1, 2, 3 and c is the speed of sound (Titze, page 153).

Titze uses c = 35,000 cm/s, which is the speed of sound in the warm, moist air of the mouth, and takes the average male vocal tract to be 17.5 cm long, which gives F(n) = (2n − 1) × 500 Hz, that is 500, 1500, 2500 and 3500 Hz, spaced 1000 Hz apart (Titze, page 159). Move the length slider in the lab below, and check that at 17.5 cm the numbers match, and compare with the 14.8 cm tube that we use for a woman's vocal tract in the other labs.

<TubeLab client:visible lang="en" />

When you choose "Show the resonance at F1 F2 F3," the picture of the tube shows the standing pressure wave at that resonance. The pressure is greatest at the closed end and zero at the open end. At F1 the pressure falls gradually from the maximum to zero along the tube. At F2 there is one still point in the middle of the tube, and at F3 there are two. Titze draws the same picture with the density of dots standing for pressure (Titze, page 153).

The numbers run from this site's code (checked with `verify.mts` against the formula by hand)

| Tube length | F1 | F2 | F3 | F4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 14.8 cm | 591 Hz | 1774 Hz | 2956 Hz | 4139 Hz |
| 15 cm | 583 Hz | 1750 Hz | 2917 Hz | 4083 Hz |
| 17.5 cm | 500 Hz | 1500 Hz | 2500 Hz | 3500 Hz |
| 20 cm | 438 Hz | 1313 Hz | 2188 Hz | 3063 Hz |

The longer the tube, the lower the formants and the closer together they are, and all of them change in the same proportion. This is the physical reason why, as Davids and LaTour say, a low larynx and rounded lips make the sound dark, and a high larynx and a wide smile make it bright (chapter 4, page 67).

## A tube open at both ends, and rounded lips

If the tube is open at both ends, the lowest resonance occurs when half a wavelength exactly fits the tube, so it is called a half-wave. The frequency is F(n) = n × c ÷ 2L, which is twice that of a tube closed at one end of the same length. Titze compares it with organ pipes: a pipe that is open at the top gives a higher note than a pipe that is closed at the top, and if you close the end of the pipe partly, the half-wave frequencies fall toward the quarter-wave ones (Titze, page 158). Rounded lips partly close the end in just this way, and so they make every formant fall, which matches what Davids and LaTour say about a long tube. We do not simulate this case in the lab. The button "Open at both ends (for comparison)" is only for comparing the formulas.

</Step>

## From formant to loudness

The vocal folds give harmonics that weaken with frequency, with a slope of about 12 dB/octave. As they pass through the vocal tract, the harmonics near a formant are raised. When all of the energy is added together, Titze concludes that the overall level is usually set by the harmonic closest to F1 (Titze, pages 255 to 257). He also finds that the radiated sound grows louder by about 8 to 9 dB per octave of rise in the fundamental (Titze, pages 254 to 258). This number explains a fact that the book by Davids and LaTour gives: a soprano sings loudest when her fundamental is amplified by F1 (chapter 4, page 67).

The lab in the lesson [Vowels and consonants](/en/singing/vowels/#adjusting-the-vowel-when-you-sing-high) shows this effect as numbers. When F1 is tuned to sit slightly above the fundamental, the synthesized sound at C6 is about 16 dB louder than in the case where F1 is left unchanged (checked with `verify.mts`). This number comes from the model, not from a measurement of singers.

</Step>

## A small tube inside a large one

Titze explains the singer's formant (which he calls the "vocal ring") as a small quarter-wave resonator sitting inside another, larger tube (Titze, page 265). The small tube is the epilarynx tube, which Davids and LaTour describe as about 2 to 3 cm long (chapter 4, page 67). With the same formula as above, a 3 cm tube resonates at about 2917 Hz, a 2.5 cm tube at 3500 Hz and a 2 cm tube at 4375 Hz. This range covers the singer's formant range that the book reports, about 2500 to 4000 Hz depending on voice type. This calculation is a comparison by the author of the lesson to show the order of magnitude, and it is not in the book directly, and a real epilarynx is not a straight tube. The narrower and shorter the tube, the higher the frequency, which matches the observation that sopranos have a ring at a higher frequency than tenors.
