## What resonance is

Resonance is the tendency of a cavity such as the mouth or the throat to reinforce some frequencies of a sound (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 63). Think of a trumpet. The vibrating lips of the player in the mouthpiece make a faint buzz, but the body of the horn reinforces certain frequencies until the sound is full and very loud. The vocal folds correspond to the lips, and the pharynx, the mouth and the part above the vocal folds correspond to the horn.

Controlling resonance lets us choose the tone color, sing louder without increasing the air pressure, make listeners feel that we sing more in tune, control a wide range of loudness, and pass through the high and low parts of the voice more easily (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 63). One caution: resonance does not amplify the sound like an electrical amplifier. It lets us change the energy of the air into sound more efficiently.

A newer line of research shows that a vocal tract that is tuned well also sends energy back to help the vocal folds vibrate (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 63). If the vocal tract is set wrongly, it can make the vibration unstable. We come back to this point in the lesson [Registers and Transitions](/en/singing/registers/#where-the-voice-changes-passaggio).

## Formants: the peaks of the filter

The vocal folds make many harmonics, but not all of them are amplified. The vocal tract has frequencies at which it resonates especially strongly, called formants. The harmonic that is amplified the most is the one close to, and below, the formant frequency (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 67). When a harmonic is in this position, the vocal tract sends energy back to the vocal folds, and the sound gets louder without more effort. If a harmonic crosses a formant frequency as the note goes higher, the vocal folds receive an unfavorable feedback, and the voice may break or change pitch suddenly.

The first three are determined mainly by the vowel (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 67).

F1 relates to the pharynx, which is the largest cavity. A large cavity resonates at low frequencies. F1 is very sensitive to lowering the back of the jaw. Lowering the jaw more reduces the volume of the pharynx near the vocal folds, and F1 goes up. In the range of speaking frequencies, F1 lies at roughly 250 to 800 Hz, and it is higher in women than in men. F1 is highest when you sing [ɑ], because the jaw is lowered the most.

F2 relates to the mouth, which is the next largest. The normal range is roughly 800 to 2200 Hz, depending on the position of the tongue and the shape of the lips. F2 is highest when the highest point of the tongue is forward and the mouth is wide, as when singing [i], and lowest when singing [u], with the tongue at the back and the mouth narrow, which is why [u] sounds darker than the other vowels.

F3 has a less clear origin. Some see it as related to the space between the front of the tongue and the lower teeth, and some as one of the resonances of the larynx. It lies at roughly 2200 to 3000 Hz, is highest in [i] and lowest in [u], and so makes [i] the "brightest" vowel, because both F2 and F3 are highest.

F4 and F5 relate to the singer's formant, which we look at below.

## A long tube makes the sound dark, a short tube makes it bright

The length of the vocal tract from the vocal folds to the lips determines every formant. If the larynx is low and the lips are rounded and protruded, the tube is longer and every formant goes down. The sound is soft and dark. If the larynx is raised and the corners of the mouth are drawn to the sides, the tube is shorter, every formant goes up, and the sound is bright (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 67). The length of the vocal tract is therefore a stylistic choice, at least in one way: classical singing wants a long tube, and belt wants a short tube for brightness.

As for tone color, in classical singing we want warmth and brightness at the same time, called chiaroscuro (light and shadow). In musical theatre and in pop or rock, brightness is the main thing, but sometimes a dark color is chosen for the performance (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 67). A study by Story, Titze and Hoffman that compared a "sleepy" (classical) way of singing with a "bright and sharp" one (probably mix belt) found that the F2 of the belt style is much higher, which makes the sound bright, and that F1 is also a little higher (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 67).

According to the book, the two styles are made of the following. Classical uses a wide pharynx, a comfortably low larynx, and a round mouth, which make the tube long. The shape of the vocal tract is like an inverted megaphone. Contemporary singing uses a narrower pharynx (close to speech), lets the larynx rise a little, and opens the mouth wide with a smile that shows many of the upper teeth, which makes the tube short. The shape is like a trumpet. It may lower the soft palate slightly so that the back of the mouth gets smaller without sounding nasal (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 83). Combining a narrow pharynx with a trumpet-shaped mouth makes it easy to sing up to about G5, after which it gets much harder, which is one reason why the mix belt of women has an upper limit at about that note.

Davids and LaTour warn that some singers in contemporary styles try to squeeze the pharynx narrow to get brightness, which increases tension and is not necessary, because lowering the soft palate slightly, raising and moving the tongue forward, letting the larynx rise by itself, and smiling widely already give brightness without tension (chapter 4, page 83, and chapter 14, page 245).

## The singer's formant: the "ring" that floats above the band

When the vocal tract is arranged suitably, F3 moves up to cluster with F4 and F5, producing strong energy in the region of about 2500 to 3200 Hz in men's voices, about 3200 Hz in contraltos and mezzos, and up to 4000 Hz in sopranos. It is called the singer's formant, or "ring." It is the reason why the voice of an opera singer can be heard over an entire orchestra, because the energy of the orchestra at 2500 to 4000 Hz is lower, and the listener's ear is most sensitive in this range (3000 to 4000 Hz). A singer who uses the singer's formant reaches a level about 15 dB higher than one who does not, and for every 6 dB of increase, the listener perceives the sound as twice as loud (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 67).

Its origin is believed to be the narrow tube about 2 to 3 cm above the vocal folds, called the epilarynx tube. A comfortably low larynx makes this tube narrow, and if the pharynx is wide enough, you get a singer's formant. If the pharynx is narrower than in classical singing, you get a "speaker's formant" that is higher. Top-level belt singers often have this kind of sound. In the lab above, adjust the "Narrow epilarynx" slider to add a bump at about 2.8 kHz and listen to whether the synthesized sound gets brighter.

A soprano who sings very high depends on the fundamental more than on the singer's formant, because the harmonics are so far apart that they do not match that cluster of formants (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 67). In a choir, someone's singer's formant can make the voice stand out so that it does not blend. Research found that singers reduce it themselves when they try to blend, especially when they sing softly.

</Step>

## What can be adjusted

The larynx moves up and down with the balance between the muscles above the larynx (swallowing, which lifts it) and below the larynx (yawning, which pulls it down). Try placing a finger on the Adam's apple and swallow, then try the start of a yawn (without yawning fully), and you will feel it go up and down. Classical or legit sound wants a comfortably low larynx, while other styles may want a higher one, but it should be an intentional choice. If you raise the larynx without realizing it when you sing high, the sound is not stable, because a singer who runs short of air may use the swallowing muscles to push the air out instead. People often think belt needs a raised larynx. In fact only the heavy chest belt raises it very high, while in mix belt the larynx stays flexible and may rise a little on high notes (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 78).

Lowering the back of the jaw and raising the soft palate make the pharynx wide for classical sound. The start of a yawn is enough. Research found that the back part of the lower jaw leaves the joint in the first 10 percent of a yawn, which lowers the larynx, stretches the pharynx, and lowers the floor of the tongue. Be careful not to jut the jaw forward: lower it and move it back a little (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 79). Raising the soft palate is a very subtle feeling. Most people find it hard to control and need to practice for a long time. The book suggests imagining that you open a paper cocktail umbrella at the back of the mouth.

The mouth is a smaller cavity than the pharynx, so it resonates at higher frequencies and makes the sound bright. The tongue determines F2 a great deal. The "inner smile" that singing teachers talk about means making space in the mouth, both horizontally and vertically, without smiling widely on the outside. For classical, a small outer smile is enough: if you see the edge of the four upper teeth when you sing [ɑ], that is fine. Contemporary singers can smile wide, and the upper teeth that show emphasize the high harmonics (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 81).

</Step>

## The misunderstanding about "placing the voice"

"Place the voice in the mask" or "sing forward" are terms used for a long time, but Davids and LaTour point out that they mislead. The nasal cavity and sinuses do not make the sound that the listener hears for ordinary vowels. In an experiment, plugging a singer's nose with cotton did not change ordinary vowels, and if you let air into the nose, F1 weakens, which makes the sound nasal and is not what you want (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 86). The vibrating feeling on the forehead is vibration carried through bone and tissue, which only the singer feels.

Manuel Garcia, who led the idea of "placing the voice" at first, changed his mind and published a retraction in 1894, writing that the idea that air is sent to strike the hard or the soft palate to get different sounds is nonsense (quoted in Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 86).

When the book looks for what "forward placement" really measures, it finds a link with a higher F2 and a strong singer's formant, which are achieved with a tongue that is high and forward and a comfortably low larynx. The advice that works in practice is therefore about the shape of the vocal tract, not about sending the sound anywhere (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 86).

Resonance also affects the perception of pitch. A singer who lacks high harmonics is perceived as singing flat even when the fundamental is right, and one who lacks low harmonics is perceived as singing sharp. A belt singer with a weak fundamental may be perceived as sharp, and needs to add a little head voice to compensate (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 85).

</Step>

## When you use a microphone

Almost every contemporary singer sings through a sound system, and there are three things worth knowing. Equalization is raising or lowering some frequency ranges, for example raising 1000 to 2200 Hz to make the sound brighter. Compression reduces the difference between loud and soft levels, which allows the overall level to be higher, but too much takes the emotion out of the song. Reverberation, or the reflection of the room, is something that listeners and singers like to have some of, but if it lasts too long the words are not clear (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 86).

</Step>

## Exercises

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: a startled breath to find the raised soft palate">
Imagine that something startles you, and breathe in quickly through the mouth. You will feel the soft palate rise. Compare it with breathing out through the nose, where you feel the soft palate lowered (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 89).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: Hang-ah">
Sing "hang" with [ŋ] and open it into [ɑ]. During the [ŋ] the soft palate is lowered and touches the back of the tongue. Put your attention on the muscles that pull the soft palate up when you change to [ɑ]. Try pinching your nose to check. If the sound changes, there is still some nasality (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 89).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: releasing the jaw, the Tetrazzini way">
Put your fingers under the temple in front of the ear and open your mouth. You will feel the jaw joint relax into a groove. That confirms that the back of the jaw is released. You do not need to open the mouth very wide. Be careful if you have a TMJ problem (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 89).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: alternate [i] and [u] on one note">
Sing [i] with a wide smile and [u] with rounded lips, alternating on a single note, and try to keep the resonance the same in both vowels. This extreme posture is for practice only and is not used in real singing. You can play the example with the guide-note player in the [Warm-up](/en/singing/warm-up-practice/) lesson (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 89).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: a pencil between the front teeth">
Place a pencil behind the lower canine teeth and let the upper teeth touch the pencil lightly. Rest the tip of the tongue on the base of the lower teeth, and sing the main vowels in the middle of your range. You will feel the vertical space in the mouth. It practices the feeling of the "inner smile" (Davids and LaTour, chapter 4, page 89).
</Callout>
