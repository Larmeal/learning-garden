## How to stand so that singing is easy

Good posture gives a singer three benefits: breathing is easier, there is less tension, and you tire more slowly (Davids and LaTour, chapter 1, page 9). When the body is out of balance, muscles that should be working on the voice have to help hold you up. People with poor posture often feel they must use more effort to sing the same sound.

The book uses the word alignment instead of posture, because the latter makes us think of standing stiffly. The position we want is balanced and flexible, and it will not be exactly the same for everyone. A starting point that works is to imagine a straight line passing behind the ear, through the middle of the shoulder, the top of the hip bone, the knee joint, and a little in front of the ankle. Place the feet about shoulder width apart, with one foot slightly in front of the other so that you do not sway from side to side. The weight is spread over both feet and does not sink into the heels or the toes too much (Davids and LaTour, chapter 1, page 9).

There are only a few details worth knowing. First, the breastbone (sternum) should be comfortably high so that it lifts the ribs and the lungs can expand, but not to the point of a military chest. Second, the head. For many styles the chin should be parallel to the floor. For classical singing, tuck the chin in slightly so that the larynx stays low and still. In belt styles, where the larynx is allowed to move up a little, the chin may be raised a little, but do not pull the chin back, which squeezes the throat, and do not push the chin forward. Third, the shoulders are relaxed in their sockets, not locked and not raised. Last, the knees must not be locked. Locked knees usually come with tense thigh muscles and a tense lower back, and they slow the return of blood, so that some people faint when they stand for a long time on a choir riser (Davids and LaTour, chapter 1, page 12, and chapter 14, page 253).

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: posture and sound">
Sing [ɑ] on one long note, then slowly pull the chin back, then push the chin forward, and then try tucking and raising the chin. Listen to how the sound changes. Doing it in front of a mirror lets you see it too. Davids and LaTour use this exercise to show that "you are your own instrument," just as we should not bend a trumpet and hope that it will play well (chapter 1, page 12).
</Callout>

## The four phases of a breath

A breath for singing has four phases. The first is breathing in. The second is a brief hold of the air (suspension). The third is breathing out while singing, and the fourth is rest (recovery), in which the muscles relax before the next cycle (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 19). ## Breathing in

The main muscles at work when you breathe in are the diaphragm and the external intercostals, the muscles between the ribs. When the diaphragm contracts and moves down, it creates suction in the lungs and air flows in. At the same time it pushes the organs of the abdomen down. If the abdominal muscles are relaxed, the diaphragm can descend fully and the lungs take in the most air, so you need to relax the abdomen and let the ribs expand to the sides as well (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 19).

The part of the abdomen that needs to relax is the part between the breastbone and the navel. The lower abdomen below the navel does not help the diaphragm descend further, and people who push the lower abdomen out often make the ribs collapse, so the lungs expand less. If you do it right, you will see the abdomen and the ribs really expand, and that is a good sign. Many people suggest imagining the back expanding a little as well, but you should not aim to expand the back so that the abdomen does not expand (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 19).

Breathing in should be quiet. If you hear the air, the throat is tight. Common causes are a soft palate that is too relaxed, a jaw that is not lowered enough, a tongue drawn back, tense muscles around the vocal tract, or vocal folds that are half closed. The remedy is to open the mouth, lower the jaw, open the gap between the vocal folds, relax the abdomen, and let the lungs fill, with the tongue and mouth already in the shape of the vowel that you are about to sing, so that the vowel does not change when the sound begins (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 19).

<Callout type="warn" title="Breathing with the upper chest (clavicular breathing)">
Some people think you must lift the chest as high as possible to fill the lungs. The result is less air, because the top of the lungs holds less than the bottom, and an abdomen that is pulled in to look slim makes it hard to control the air going out. The shoulders rise with it and the neck gets tense (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 19). Press the "Upper chest" button in the animation to compare. If you are a dancer, or have just been running, the body may switch to this kind of breathing by itself without your knowing.
</Callout>

Fill with air, but not all the way. Richard Miller (quoted in Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 19) says that the lungs should not feel packed, they should feel adequate, because if you stuff in too much air the rate at which the air flows out gets faster, like an overfilled balloon that shoots its air out. The sound turns breathy and bubbly, and the neck, chest and larynx get tense as well.

</Step>

## A short hold before you sing

After you breathe in and before the sound begins, the body has a short moment to change from the muscles of breathing in to the muscles of breathing out. In everyday life we hardly pause here, but in singing this short moment helps you begin with the right pressure, and the vocal folds close in a relaxed way by themselves, naturally. If you practice holding for too long, beginners tend to tense the larynx and the neck and close the vocal folds too firmly, which leads to a hard onset (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 24). The benefit of practicing the hold is to practice keeping the expanded position of the abdomen and ribs, not to hold your breath.

</Step>

## Breathing out while singing, and the word Appoggio

The term "breath support" means sending just the right air pressure to the vocal folds. There are two groups of muscles at work when you breathe out: the internal intercostals and the abdominal muscles (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 25). When these two groups contract, the organs of the abdomen push the diaphragm up and the air leaves the lungs. In one study, the people who were rated as having the best voice and vibrato used the abdominal muscles more than the rib muscles.

The key is to resist the ribs and abdomen collapsing quickly until the end of the phrase. The muscles of breathing in and breathing out must work in balance, for example the external intercostals resist the internal intercostals a little all the time while we let the air out. An Italian teacher named Lamperti called this living tension appoggio, from a word meaning "to lean" or "to support." At the start, wake up the abdominal muscles gently, so that you feel the abdomen push out just a little without the muscles moving much. If you rest your fingers on the abdomen between the breastbone and the navel, you will feel the muscles working but still springy, like a firm mattress (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 25).

Why resist? The physics is simple: compressed air in the lungs always pushes back. When the lungs are full the pushback is strong, and as the air runs out the pushback gets lighter. If you let the ribs and abdomen collapse naturally, the pressure at the vocal folds is too high at the start of the phrase and drops steadily until you run out of strength at the end. Titze relates that singers therefore need a mental picture of "breathing in at the same time as breathing out," and there are reports that the diaphragm keeps working after the breathing-in phase, to compensate for the recoil pressure at the start of the phrase (Titze, page 80). Go back to the animation above and compare the graph between "let it collapse" and "resist it." That picture is a sketch for explanation, not measured data.

Another benefit is that appoggio reduces tension in the vocal tract. Many singers who run short of air squeeze the muscles around the throat to push the air out, especially at the end of a phrase, which makes the sound tense, high notes hard, and long sounds unsteady (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 25).

## Common misunderstandings and numbers worth knowing

The diaphragm is not a muscle that we control directly when we breathe out. You should not order yourself to "sing from the diaphragm" or "bounce the diaphragm" in staccato. In trained singers it has been reported that the diaphragm is held back from rising quickly, which gives finer control of the pressure, and that the trachea is pulled down gently (tracheal pull), which lowers the larynx and reduces the squeezing of the vocal folds together on high notes (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 25).

There are three numbers worth knowing. First, the louder or higher you sing, the more air pressure (below the vocal folds) you need. Doubling the pressure makes the sound about twice as loud. A tenor who moves from A3 to E4 at a medium loudness needs to increase the pressure by about 150 percent. Second, high notes do not need more "volume of air." If you push out a lot of air on high notes, the sound gets poor and goes out of tune. Third, you must prepare the pressure before you leap from a low note to a high one, not add it after you have arrived on the high note, otherwise you will land under the target or have to slide up to it (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 29).

If you sing while dancing, or just after hard exertion, the time you can sing a long sound falls from an average of about 20 seconds to about 7 seconds, in a study by Sliiden and colleagues that the book cites (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 31). Long phrases therefore need the intensity of the choreography to be reduced.

</Step>

## Rest, then breathe again

The recovery phase should be an intentional part of breathing. The muscles used to breathe, the muscles of the larynx, and the muscles that shape vowels and consonants (tongue, jaw, lips) need time to relax, or tension builds up. If the phrase is long and the voice is high, adding a half-beat or a beat of rest helps. In a choir, use staggered breathing, choosing a point where it does not disturb the line, such as the middle of a long note, and come back in slightly lighter than before (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 30).

</Step>

## Exercises

The exercises below come from Davids and LaTour, chapter 2 (pages 32 to 36). Breathing practice is often the first thing cut from a warm-up, because it feels like something we already do every day. The book warns that it takes months to years before it becomes automatic, so you should not give up too early (page 36). {/* ref-ok */}

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: breathing lying down">
Lie on your back with a book under your head. Think of a sleeping baby: the abdomen rises when you breathe in. Put a light book on your abdomen to see when it moves. Let the abdomen and ribs expand. The chest may expand a little after the abdomen has risen, which is evidence that the lungs are filling correctly. Do this for a couple of minutes, then stand up and repeat it, checking that the abdomen and lower ribs expand as you breathe in.
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: hiss on [s] to practice controlling the air going out (sibilant exercise)">
Put one hand on the abdomen at the navel and the other at your side under the ribs. Breathe in quietly and then hiss a gentle, steady [s]. Try not to let the abdomen and ribs collapse until the very end, and keep the vocal folds open the whole time. (If you change it to [v], the vocal folds vibrate, which is closer to singing.) You can time it with the lab above and see whether the next attempt lasts longer, without forcing it into tension (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 32).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: the quick snap breath">
Before a phrase where there is little time to breathe, let the abdomen spring out by itself to draw air in quickly, then control the air going out as you do in the hissing exercise. The inhalation must stay quiet, because a fast intake often comes with a tight throat (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 32).
</Callout>

<Callout type="try" title="Try it: lip trill and tongue trill to check whether the support slips">
A trill resists the outgoing air strongly, so it needs steady pressure. If the support slips, the trill breaks or the sound changes. Try sliding up and down a fifth, or an arpeggio, with this method (Davids and LaTour, chapter 2, page 32). We use trills again in the lesson [Vocal Folds and Onset](/en/singing/vocal-folds/) and in the [warm-up](/en/singing/warm-up-practice/) plan.
</Callout>
