## Most bad data makes no noise

The preface of *Data Quality Fundamentals* opens with events that anyone who works with data has probably met: an important table that normally holds five thousand rows drops to five hundred for no reason, an executive dashboard fills up with nulls, or a schema changes quietly until a downstream pipeline breaks.

The authors call a period when data is incomplete, wrong or missing **data downtime**, by analogy with the downtime of an online service. They point out that on many teams the problem is first noticed by the downstream consumer when a report looks "off", not by the team that built the pipeline.

This path is about making your team the one that finds out first.

## Quality means "fit for use"

DMBOK2 defines it this way: data is of high quality to the extent that it **meets the expectations and needs of the data consumer**, meaning it fits the purpose the consumer will use it for. So quality depends on context and on the consumer (book: DMBOK2 §1.3.1).

Look at it with our dataset (500 customers, 3,020 order rows, 2,553 payments). Two consumers read the same data, and each has their own line for what is acceptable.

| Consumer | Needs | Good | Total | Score | Acceptable line | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marketing | Customers have an email that can be sent to | 445 | 500 | 89.0% | 98.0% | Not fit |
| Finance | Order amounts match the payment system | 2,498 | 2,533 | 98.6% | 99.9% | Not fit |

(ran: `01_fit_for_use.py`. The email numbers match what was planted: 40 blank plus 15 badly formatted makes 55 customers.)

The 98% line for marketing is taken from the example in DMBOK2 §1.3.3, which aims for a usable email for at least 98% of customers. The 99.9% for finance is **a number the author picked**, not a standard.

Two things to notice:

1. The `customers` table that is "not fit" for marketing has nothing to do with finance. So the first question is "who uses it, and what do they need?", not "is the data clean?"
2. There is no ready-made formula for the acceptable line. The consumer has to tell you. DMBOK2 §2.1 suggests asking stakeholders directly: "what is your tolerance for low-quality data?"

## Not all data matters equally

DMBOK2 gives the principle of **criticality**: a data quality program should focus on the data most important to the organization and its customers, and prioritize by the risk when the data is wrong (book: DMBOK2 §1.2). Data that is usually counted as important is data needed for:

- regulatory reports
- financial reports
- business policy
- day-to-day operations
- competitive strategy

(book: DMBOK2 §1.3.2)

The result is a list of data ordered by importance. The team uses that list to decide where to start checking (book: DMBOK2 §2.3). Data that nobody uses to make decisions is not worth dozens of rules.

</Step>

## Assessment, control and assurance

These three words get mixed up. This path uses them this way:

| Word | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Assessment** | Looking at how good the data is today. The result is a plan | First profiling and first check against rules (lesson 3) |
| **Control** | One mechanism that prevents or checks at one point | A constraint when data is entered, a check inside a pipeline (lesson 5) |
| **Assurance** | The whole system that keeps you confident over time | Rules, measurement, monitoring, incident response, contracts, reports (lessons 4 to 10) |

**Worth knowing:** this split of three words is **the framing of this path's author**. The book does not divide it this way. DMBOK2 uses the umbrella term Data Quality Management and lists these activities in order (book: DMBOK2 §2.1 to §2.7):

1. Define what good data means
2. Set a strategy
3. Identify critical data and business rules
4. Do an initial quality assessment
5. Prioritize what to improve
6. Set goals
7. Develop and run **data quality operations**: manage rules, measure and monitor, manage issues, set SLAs, report

Activity 7 is what this path calls assurance. Activities 1 to 6 are the foundation that has to exist first.

</Step>

## The price of bad data

The authors of *Data Quality Fundamentals* offer a formula for the cost of one bad-data incident, borrowing the idea from DevOps work (book: DQF ch.5).

```text
(TTD hours + TTR hours) × cost per hour of downtime = cost of the incident
```

- **TTD** (time to detection): the time until the team knows there is a problem
- **TTR** (time to resolution): the time from knowing to finishing the fix

A made-up example from this path's author: 30 hours to notice, 6 hours to fix, 300 per hour gives (30 + 6) × 300 = 10,800 per incident (ran: `01_fit_for_use.py`, which is only arithmetic. All three inputs are assumptions.)

The book says many teams have a TTD of days, weeks or even months, because downstream consumers are the ones who find the problem first, and the longer it takes, the harder it is to recover the data by rerunning or backfilling. That is why assurance invests in **finding out early** (lesson 7).

<Callout type="warn" title="Numbers from a vendor">
The DQF authors founded a company that sells data observability tools. The book cites survey results, such as teams spending 30 to 40% of their time fixing data quality problems, and a Gartner estimate of yearly cost. Read these as **the authors' claims**, not verified facts. The cost formula is a useful way of thinking, but the numbers you put in must come from your own team.
</Callout>

## Prevent first, fix at the root

DMBOK2 says plainly that the best approach is to keep bad data from entering the organization, and that "inspecting data after it is in production does not improve its quality" (book: DMBOK2 §4.1). When a problem has already happened, the best practice is to "fix it where the problem occurs", and the fix should include preventing it from recurring (book: DMBOK2 §4.2). On cost, the book says prevention is usually cheaper than correction, "sometimes by several times" (book: DMBOK2 §2.6).

In our dataset 8% of emails are blank. If we just fill the values or delete the rows every night, the problem comes back tomorrow. We have to ask how the sign-up form or the source system allowed it to be blank (lesson 9 is about tracing the root cause).

</Callout>
