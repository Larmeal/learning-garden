## What the word asks

DMBOK2 defines validity as whether data values are consistent with a defined domain of values. The domain can be a defined set of valid values such as a reference table, a range, or a value that a rule can determine. The data type, the format and the precision all go into the definition of the domain (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459). DAMA UK puts it more briefly: the data conforms to the syntax (format, type, range) of its definition (book: DMBOK2 section 1.3.3, page 457).

Think of a guest list at a door. The person checking the list can tell you that a name is on it. They cannot tell you that the person in front of them is who they claim to be. Validity is the first check only.

</Step>

## Three kinds of domain, on real rows

| Kind of domain | Rule in our data | Fails | Example rows that fail |
|---|---|---:|---|
| A list | `country` is one of TH, SG, JP, US | 25 of 500 | 36 `THA`, 101 `T.H.`, 135 `Thailand`, 138 `ไทย` |
| A range | `birth_date` between 1900-01-01 and today | 6 of 500 | 367 `1890-05-01`, 80 `2030-01-01` |
| A range | `total_amount` is not negative | 12 of 3,020 | order 321 `-187.35`, order 436 `-94.01` |
| A pattern | `email` looks like `a@b.c` (non-null rows) | 15 of 460 | 23 `user23example.com`, 88 `user88example.com` |

```sql
SELECT count(*) FILTER (WHERE country NOT IN ('TH','SG','JP','US')), count(*) FROM customers;
-- 25 of 500
```

Every one of these numbers equals what the generator planted (run: `02_dimensions.py`). The rows that fail do so in different ways. Some are rubbish (`1850-01-01`), some are a different spelling of something real (`ไทย`), and one is possibly a legitimate row, which the next step comes back to.

</Step>

## Meanings that get misread

<Callout type="warn" title="1. Valid is not accurate">
Of the 35 orders whose total differs from the payment system, all 35 pass `total_amount >= 0`. Of the 20 customers who share an email with someone else, all 20 have an email of the correct shape (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). A validity score of 100% says that nothing impossible got in. It does not say that what got in is right. DMBOK2 warns about the same thing: data may be valid and still not be accurate or correctly associated with particular records (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459).
</Callout>

**2. A loose rule accepts rubbish.** The pattern `^[^@\s]+@[^@\s]+\.[^@\s]+$` accepts `a@b.c` and `nobody@nowhere.zz`. On a made-up table of 6 emails it accepts 4, among them `a@b.c` and `x@y.z` (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). Passing means "has the shape of an email". Whether the mailbox exists is another question, and only a confirmation email answers it.

**3. The rule is a statement of the business, not of the data.** We treat a negative `total_amount` as a defect, which gives 12 rows. If the shop records refunds as negative orders, those rows are legitimate and the rule is wrong. The data cannot settle this. The owner of the data can. The same goes for the lower bound of 1900 on `birth_date`: it is a choice, and a customer born in 1899 would fail a rule that is no more than a convention. Say who wrote each rule down and who agreed to it (the rule choices are the author's).

**4. A date can be valid in two ways at once.** `09/05/2026` is 9 May if day comes first and 5 September if month comes first. On a made-up table of 3 date strings, a day-first parser accepts 2 and a month-first parser accepts 2, and the string `09/05/2026` is accepted by both while meaning different days (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). Ten Steps tells you to be clear on date formats for exactly this reason (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 128 to 135).

**5. Validity can expire.** The book notes that some data is valid only for a specific length of time, such as data from RFID tags or some scientific data sets (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459). A rule that held last year can fail this year without a single row changing.

**6. An off-list value can be a separate problem.** `ไทย` is outside the list, so it fails validity. It also writes the same country that `TH` writes, which is a consistency problem. You can reject it, or map it to `TH`. The measurement does not choose. See [the consistency page](/en/data-quality/dimensions/consistency/#three-levels).

</Step>

## Easy to confuse with

| Looks like | How to tell them apart | In our data |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Validity checks against a rule. Accuracy checks against the real thing or a trusted source | 35 wrong totals, all valid |
| Consistency | Validity needs a defined domain. Consistency needs two representations to compare | `ไทย` is invalid against the list and inconsistent with `TH` |
| Completeness | A NULL is missing, not invalid | 40 NULL emails are not counted in the 15 of 460 |

On the last row: we leave NULL out of the validity denominator on purpose, so one blank email fails completeness once and not twice. Ten Steps reports validity this way, as in "95% fill rate, and of the records with a value, 90% conform" (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 128 to 135).

</Step>

## Check yourself

- Write down the domain of one column you use: is it a list, a range or a pattern? Where does the definition live, and who changed it last?
- Your validity score for `email` is 100% after you tighten the rule. What would you still not know about the column?
- A rule fails 12 rows. What do you ask before you call them defects?

</Step>
