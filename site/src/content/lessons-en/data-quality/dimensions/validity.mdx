## Validity asks whether the value in a field is one the field allows

Picture a person at the door of an event holding a guest list. They can check whether the name a guest gives is on the list. They cannot check whether the person standing in front of them really is that guest. Validity does the job of the person with the list: it checks whether a value meets the rules of its field, and that is only the first gate.

Here are five values that their fields do not accept, taken from our dataset.

| Table | Row | Field | Value | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| customers | 23 | email | `user23example.com` | no `@` |
| customers | 36 | country | THA | the system accepts only TH, SG, JP and US |
| customers | 367 | birth_date | 1890-05-01 | older than 1900, the lower bound we set |
| customers | 28 | birth_date | 2026-12-25 | a day in the future |
| orders | 321 | total_amount | -187.35 | a negative total |

DMBOK2 defines validity as whether data values match a defined domain. The domain is the set of values the field accepts. It can be a list of values, such as countries, a range, such as birth dates, or a pattern the value must follow, such as an email. Data type, format and precision are part of the domain too. DAMA UK says it more briefly: the data conforms to the syntax (format, type, range) of its definition (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459, and section 1.3.3, page 457).

The five examples above cover all three kinds of domain.

| Kind of domain | Rule in our dataset | Fails |
|---|---|---:|
| A list | `country` is TH, SG, JP or US | 25 of 500 |
| A range | `birth_date` is between 1900-01-01 and today | 6 of 500 |
| A range | `total_amount` is not negative | 12 of 3,020 |
| A pattern | `email` looks like `a@b.c` (only rows where email is not empty) | 15 of 460 |

```sql
SELECT count(*) FILTER (WHERE country NOT IN ('TH','SG','JP','US')), count(*) FROM customers;
-- 25 of 500
```

Every number in the table matches what was planted (run: `02_dimensions.py`).

## A value can pass the rule and still be wrong

Go back to the person with the guest list. If the name "Somchai" is on the list, they let the person in, even if whoever stands there is someone else using that name. Our data has the same situation.

Order 91 records a total of 487.71, but the payment system, which received the money, has 437.71. The total is wrong by 50. But 487.71 is a positive number, so it passes the rule `total_amount >= 0` easily. Of the 35 orders whose total does not match the payment system, all 35 pass that rule.

Customer 1 and customer 491 share the email `user1@example.com`, which is a perfectly well formed email. Of the 20 customers who share an email with someone else, all 20 have an email that passes the format rule (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`).

So a validity score of 100% tells us only that nothing impossible got in. It does not tell us that what got in is right. DMBOK2 warns about the same thing: data can pass its domain and still not be accurate, or be tied to the wrong record (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459). Finding out whether a value is really right is the job of [Accuracy](/en/data-quality/dimensions/accuracy/).

</Step>

## A rule that is too loose accepts junk

The email rule we use says that an email has some characters, then `@`, then some characters, then a dot, then more characters. That accepts some strange emails. This table lists 6 made-up emails that we ran through the rule.

| email | Does the rule accept it? |
|---|---|
| `a@b.c` | accepted |
| `x@y.z` | accepted |
| `nobody@nowhere.zz` | accepted |
| `ann@example.com` | accepted |
| `ann@@example.com` | rejected (two `@`) |
| `ann@example` | rejected (no dot) |

The rule accepts 4 of the 6, and among those four are `a@b.c` and `x@y.z`, which look like emails but probably belong to nobody (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). Passing the rule only means the value looks like an email. Whether a mailbox exists there is something we find out only by sending mail. If we make the rule stricter, it starts rejecting emails that are valid, so we have to balance the two to suit the task.

</Step>

<Step>

## Who decides which values are acceptable

A validity rule is a business agreement that we write down as code. Are the 12 rows with a negative total in `orders` defects? If the shop records refunds as orders with negative totals, those rows are correct, and our rule is the thing that is wrong. The data cannot tell us which is true. Only the owner of the data can.

The lower bound of 1900 on `birth_date` is an agreement too. It removes customer 367, born in 1890, which is sensible, but if a customer born in 1899 ever appeared, they would fail a rule that is only our convention. It is worth writing down who wrote each rule and who agreed to it (the choice of rules is the author's).

Validity can also expire. The book notes that some data is valid only for a certain length of time, such as data from RFID tags or some scientific datasets. A rule that passed last year can fail this year without any row changing (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459).

</Step>

## The same date can be read two ways

`09/05/2026` is 9 May if the day comes first, and 5 September if the month comes first. The table below shows 3 made-up strings and how two parsers read them.

| Text | Read as day first | Read as month first |
|---|---|---|
| 09/05/2026 | passes (9 May) | passes (5 September) |
| 13/05/2026 | passes (13 May) | fails (there is no month 13) |
| 05/13/2026 | fails (there is no month 13) | passes (13 May) |

Each parser passes 2 of the 3, and the first row passes both ways although the two readings mean different days (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). A rule that checks the date format therefore cannot tell us whether the date is the right one, until we have agreed which format belongs to the field. Ten Steps says to be explicit about date formats for the same reason (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 128 to 135).

</Step>

<Step>

## How it differs from the other dimensions

Validity differs from completeness in that validity checks values that exist, while completeness checks whether a value exists at all. Customer 14 has no email, which is completeness. Customer 23 has an email in the wrong format, which is validity. That is why the denominator of the email format rule is 460 and not 500: we leave out the empty emails, so one empty cell does not fail two rules at once. Ten Steps reports it the same way, for example a fill rate of 95%, and among the rows that have a value, 90% match the right pattern (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 128 to 135).

Validity differs from accuracy in the way the previous section showed. Between validity and consistency there are cases that collide. A customer whose country is written `ไทย` fails the allowed list, which is validity, and it names the same country that `TH` names, which is a consistency problem as well. We can reject the value or map it to `TH`, and the measurement cannot choose for us. See the page [Consistency](/en/data-quality/dimensions/consistency/).

</Step>
