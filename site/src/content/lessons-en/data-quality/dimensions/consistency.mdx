## What the word asks

DMBOK2 describes consistency as values being represented the same way within a data set and between data sets, and being associated the same way across data sets. It can also mean the size and composition of data sets across systems or across time, and it can mean consistency of format (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458). The DAMA UK wording is the absence of difference, when comparing two or more representations of a thing against a definition (book: DMBOK2 section 1.3.3, page 457).

Two clocks hang in one house. If they show the same time, they are consistent. If one is ten minutes fast, they are not. Neither fact tells you what time it is. The last sentence of the book's description says the same: take care not to confuse consistency with accuracy or correctness (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458).

</Step>

## Three levels

The book names three levels (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458):

| Level | The comparison | Our rule | Fails |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Record-level | Two attributes in the same row | a SHIPPED order must have `shipped_ts` | 25 of 1,794 |
| Record-level | Two attributes in the same row | `shipped_ts` is not before `order_ts` | 10 of 1,769 |
| Cross-record | One attribute across different rows or data sets | the same country written the same way in every row | 25 rows in five other spellings |
| Temporal | One attribute at different points in time | today's order count is near the count of recent days | see [the reasonability page](/en/data-quality/dimensions/reasonability/#meanings-that-get-misread) |

Rows that fail the first two rules:

| order_id | status | order_ts | shipped_ts |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 84 | SHIPPED | 2026-07-03 06:01 | NULL |
| 816 | SHIPPED | 2026-10-02 03:26 | 2026-10-01 03:26 |

In order 816, each timestamp is a valid timestamp. The order was shipped a day before it was placed. The defect exists only in the pair, which is why no per-column check can find it. Cross-record consistency looks like this in `customers.country`:

| country | rows |
|---|---:|
| TH | 330 |
| SG | 58 |
| JP | 56 |
| US | 31 |
| THA, T.H., th, Thailand, ไทย | 5 each |

Six spellings stand for one country (run: `02_dimensions.py`, `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`).

</Step>

## Meanings that get misread

<Callout type="warn" title="1. Agreement does not mean correct">
Take three orders. The `total_amount` column equals the copy of the total in a report table for all 3, and equals what the payment system recorded for 1 of 3 (a made-up table, run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). A copy always agrees with the thing it was copied from. If two systems share a bug, every consistency check between them passes. Consistency tells you where to look. Accuracy needs a trusted source.
</Callout>

**2. The measure reports a difference. A definition decides who is right.** "Six spellings of Thailand" is a finding. Choosing `TH` and mapping the other five to it is standardization, which the book describes as conditioning input data so that it meets rules for content and format (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458). The measurement cannot make that choice for you, which is why the DAMA UK wording says "against a definition".

**3. Each column can look healthy.** Profile `order_ts` and `shipped_ts` one at a time ([lesson 3](/en/data-quality/profiling/#profile-every-column-without-knowing-what-it-means)) and both look fine. Record-level rules need you to know the business relation between two columns, and that relation often lives in someone's head. Ask for it.

**4. A rule that finds nothing is still information.** "A PLACED order has no `shipped_ts`" fails 0 rows in our data (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). You learn that the system does not leak shipped times into unshipped orders. Keep the rule. It will notice if that changes.

**5. The same rule can have a different home.** Ten Steps lists "order date must always be before ship date" under referential integrity, because it treats integrity in a general way as consistency of data within a relationship (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 128 to 135). DMBOK2 files it under consistency. Both are defensible. The rule is the same and only the label differs, so agree on one label for your reports.

**6. The book also puts "over time" here.** Its example is a rule that the number of transactions in a day should not exceed 105% of the running average of the previous 30 days (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458). Many teams call that volume monitoring or reasonability. [The reasonability page](/en/data-quality/dimensions/reasonability/#meanings-that-get-misread) shows what such a bar does on our data.

</Step>

## Easy to confuse with

| Looks like | How to tell them apart | In our data |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Consistency compares two representations. Accuracy compares with reality or an authority | total matches its own copy 3 of 3, matches payments 1 of 3 |
| Validity | A value inside the list can still contradict another column | order 816, both timestamps valid |
| Reconciliation | Reconciliation is consistency between two systems, measured as a process ([lesson 8](/en/data-quality/reconciliation/#reconcile-with-row-counts-and-sums)) | order totals against payment totals |

Ten Steps also treats consistency across data stores as its own dimension, "consistency and synchronization", so that you plan time for profiling every store and comparing (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 146 to 148).

</Step>
