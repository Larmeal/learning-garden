## Reasonability asks whether the pattern in the data looks the way we expect

Think of a shop owner looking at the till roll at closing time. Every receipt looks normal, and none of them is wrong. But the total for the day is 1% of an ordinary Tuesday, and the owner knows at once that something is wrong, without reading a single receipt, because they already had an expectation of where the total should be. Reasonability turns that expectation into a number we can check.

DMBOK2 says reasonability asks whether a data pattern meets expectations. The book's example is whether the distribution of sales across a geographic area makes sense, given what we know about the customers in that area. It can be measured in several ways, such as comparing with benchmark data or with past instances of a similar dataset, for example sales from the previous quarter. Once benchmark measurements are agreed, we can compare them with a new dataset to detect change (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459).

Ten Steps gives smaller examples of the same idea inside a row or a column. One is a birth date and an age that disagree, such as an applicant who must be 18 or older whose dates make them 2 years old. Another is the distribution of country codes that does not match what we expect to sell in each country (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 128 to 135).

</Step>

## A bad day that no row-level rule can see

Our dataset has no planted defect of this kind, so we created one ourselves in a copy of `orders`. We divided the totals of every order on 30 August by 100, as if one source had sent the smaller unit instead of the whole unit. The original data is untouched, and this is a made-up situation (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`).

Let us compare two kinds of rule against this copy.

| Check | Result on the modified copy |
|---|---|
| Validity: `total_amount >= 0` | 0 rows fail, because every total is still a positive number |
| Reasonability: the average total of the day is far from usual | 1 day flagged |

On the original data the average order total per day is about 2,537, and it varies by about 304 around that value (that is the standard deviation, which says how far the daily numbers normally spread from the average). In the copy, 30 August has an average total of only 27.89.

The rule we use is "flag a day whose average is more than 3 standard deviations from the mean of the daily averages". It flags 1 day on the copy and 0 days on the original data.

```sql
SELECT CAST(order_ts AS DATE) AS d, count(*) AS n, avg(total_amount) AS avg_total
FROM (SELECT DISTINCT * FROM orders WHERE total_amount >= 0)
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;
-- then compare each day's avg_total with its own history
```

This check cannot be written as a filter on a single row. It needs an aggregate over many rows and a baseline from other days. In practice it therefore belongs to the monitoring work that [lesson 7](/en/data-quality/monitoring/#volume-seasonality-fools-the-z-score) teaches.

</Step>

<Step>

## A threshold built from the average alone fires on normal days

The book's example bar for consistency over time is a day above 105% of the average of the previous 30 days (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458). Let us apply that bar to our daily order count, which has an average of 24.7 orders and moves up and down by about 4.8 orders.

The bar fires on 34 of the 91 days that have a full 30 days of history, although nothing is wrong on those days. The daily order count already varies naturally, and 5% is a much narrower band than that variation. A good bar therefore also needs the standard deviation, for example "more than 3 standard deviations from the mean". The 105% in the book illustrates the idea, and cannot be used as it stands everywhere (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`).

</Step>

<Step>

## "Makes sense" has to be written as a number

"Makes sense" is only somebody's opinion until we write it down as a number. The book says some expectations may seem subjective, and that we should work with the consumers of the data to find out what they expect and on what basis, and then turn that into a concrete comparison (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459).

The question to ask them is what a person who knows the business would expect to see here, and how far away counts as too far. Their answer is the bar we use.

</Step>

<Step>

## The baseline must be trustworthy, and a result is a signal for a person to look

If the history we use to calculate the average contains bad days, the normal band gets wider and we will miss the next bad day. In the example above, the single bad day out of 121 raised the standard deviation from 304 to 379 when it was counted in its own history. It was still caught, because it was so far out. We should build the baseline from data we have already checked, and update it on purpose.

When a rule fires, we should treat it as a signal for a person to look, because we cannot yet conclude that the data is wrong. A total that is wrong but plausible passes the bar: one that is 10% too high is still well inside the usual band. A real event such as a promotion day sits far from the average and makes the bar fire, even though the data is right (this remark is the author's).

</Step>

<Step>

## How it differs from the other dimensions

Reasonability differs from [Validity](/en/data-quality/dimensions/validity/) in that validity judges one value against a domain, while reasonability judges the pattern across many values. Our bad day passes validity completely.

The temporal consistency example in the book (the 105% rule), mentioned on the page [Consistency](/en/data-quality/dimensions/consistency/#three-levels), is a reasonability check under another name.

A pipeline that is silent for a whole day makes the table look like a shop that was very quiet. That is a [Timeliness](/en/data-quality/dimensions/timeliness/) problem, but reasonability catches it too.

It is the only dimension in this path that has no planted defect in the dataset. The other seven have a count that the script checks. This one has a made-up day and a threshold, which is weaker evidence, and we say so plainly.

</Step>
