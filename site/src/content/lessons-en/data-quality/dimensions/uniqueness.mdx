## What the word asks

DMBOK2 says uniqueness means that no entity exists more than once within the data set. That implies a key value relates to each entity, and only to that entity. The book's advice for measuring it is to test against key structure (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459). DAMA UK words it as: no entity instance will be recorded more than once, based upon how that thing is identified (book: DMBOK2 section 1.3.3, page 457).

The key is doing a lot of work in that sentence. A uniqueness check can only find what the key can see. Picture a class register where students are listed by seat number. The register has no repeated seat numbers, and the same student can still sit in two seats under two spellings of their name.

</Step>

<Step>

## Three different things called "a duplicate"

Ten Steps keeps three words apart because tools and people use "matching" and "duplicate" loosely (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 140 to 146):

| Term | Meaning | In our data |
|---|---|---|
| Duplicate record | Two records that are exact copies | Order 267 appears twice, identical in every column |
| Matching records | Records with a high degree of similarity | `user1@example.com` appears on customer 1 and on customer 491 |
| Equivalent records | Records that refer to the same real-world entity | Are customers 1 and 491 one person or two? The data cannot say |

| order_id | customer_id | status | total_amount |
|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 267 | 360 | PLACED | 4383.96 |
| 267 | 360 | PLACED | 4383.96 |

| customer_id | email | country |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | user1@example.com | SG |
| 491 | user1@example.com | TH |

The first pair is a plain defect. The second pair is a question. Same email, different country, different `customer_id`. It could be one person who signed up twice, or a family that shares a mailbox. Ten Steps adds a warning: similarity measures give a likelihood that records are duplicates, not proof, and very similar records can belong to different people (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 140 to 146).

</Step>

## Measuring it

```sql
-- extra rows beyond the first copy of each order_id
SELECT count(*) - count(DISTINCT order_id), count(*) FROM orders;
-- 20 of 3,020

-- the same for email, over non-null rows
SELECT count(email) - count(DISTINCT email), count(email) FROM customers;
-- 10 of 460
```

Both counts equal what was planted (run: `02_dimensions.py`). The first one is 99.34% unique and the second 97.83%.

</Step>

## Meanings that get misread

<Callout type="warn" title="1. Which number do you report?">
Order duplicates can be counted two ways. There are 20 extra rows, which is what a cleanup would delete. There are also 40 rows that belong to a duplicate group, which is how many rows someone has to look at (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). Both are honest. Reporting "20 of 3,020" in one place and "40 of 3,020" in another looks like the data changed. Pick one, write it in the rule, and keep the other as a note.
</Callout>

**2. A unique key does not make unique entities.** `customer_id` is unique over all 500 rows, and 460 non-null emails hold only 450 distinct values (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). A key rule on `customer_id` passes. Which column or combination defines "the same customer" is a business decision, and the check should be written against that choice.

**3. Exact text hides near copies.** On a made-up table of 4 emails (`ann@example.com`, `Ann@Example.com`, `ann@example.com ` with a trailing space, and `bob@example.com`), `count(DISTINCT email)` says 4. After `lower(trim(email))` it says 2 (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). Standardizing before comparing finds more, and the book makes the same point when it says tools differ on whether they standardize first (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 140 to 146).

**4. `SELECT DISTINCT` can hide the defect instead of fixing it.** Summing `total_amount` over `orders` gives 7,616,911.36. After removing the exact copies it is 7,560,842.09, so the duplicates overstate revenue by 56,069.27 (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). A model that applies `DISTINCT` gives the right revenue, and a uniqueness check placed after it will pass. The source is still emitting duplicates, and every other reader of the raw table still gets the wrong total. Check before the cleanup, not after.

**5. The same defect can have two names.** DMBOK2 lists duplicates under integrity as well: identical rows that may negatively affect aggregation (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459). You can file a repeated `order_id` under uniqueness or under integrity. What matters is that one team does not count it in both.

</Step>

## Easy to confuse with

| Looks like | How to tell them apart | In our data |
|---|---|---|
| Integrity | Integrity looks for keys that point at nothing. Uniqueness looks for keys that appear too often | 30 orphan orders against 20 extra order rows |
| Consistency | Two spellings of one customer are a consistency problem until you decide they are one entity | `TH` and `ไทย` in `country`, and `Ann@Example.com` in `email` |
| Validity | A duplicate can be perfectly valid | all 20 customers who share an email have a well-formed address |

</Step>
