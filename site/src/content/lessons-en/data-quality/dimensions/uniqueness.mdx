## Uniqueness asks whether each thing is recorded only once

Think of a class register that identifies students by seat number. The register has no repeated seat numbers, but the same student could be sitting in two seats under two slightly different spellings of their name. Checking that seat numbers are not repeated therefore cannot tell us that students are not repeated. Uniqueness works the same way: we can check only what the key can see.

DMBOK2 says uniqueness means that no entity exists more than once in the table. That implies one key value points to one entity, and only that one, and the book's advice for measuring it is to test against the key structure. DAMA UK writes it as: no entity instance is recorded more than once, based on how that thing is identified (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459, and section 1.3.3, page 457).

Our dataset has two cases that bring this to mind. The first is in `orders`.

| order_id | customer_id | status | total_amount |
|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 267 | 360 | PLACED | 4383.96 |
| 267 | 360 | PLACED | 4383.96 |

Order 267 is in two rows that are identical in every column. If we add up sales directly, this order is counted twice. The second case is in `customers`.

| customer_id | email | country |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | `user1@example.com` | SG |
| 491 | `user1@example.com` | TH |

These two customers have different `customer_id` values but share one email.

</Step>

## "Duplicate" has three meanings

The two cases above are not duplicates in the same way. Ten Steps keeps three terms apart, because tools and people use "match" and "duplicate" interchangeably (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 140 to 146).

| Term | Meaning | In our dataset |
|---|---|---|
| Duplicate record | Two records that are exact copies | order 267 in the two rows above |
| Matching records | Records that are very similar | customers 1 and 491, who share an email |
| Equivalent records | Records that refer to the same thing in the real world | whether customers 1 and 491 are one person or two, the data cannot say |

The first pair is a plain defect, because order 267 should not have two rows. The second pair is a question. The email is the same, but the country is different and so is the `customer_id`. It could be one person who registered twice, or two people in a family sharing a mailbox. Ten Steps adds a warning: similarity measures give only a likelihood that records are duplicates, not proof, and two very similar records can still be different people (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 140 to 146).

So we should not delete or merge customers 1 and 491 the moment we find them. We first ask someone who knows the customers whether they are the same person.

</Step>

## Measuring it

```sql
-- extra rows beyond the first copy of each order_id
SELECT count(*) - count(DISTINCT order_id), count(*) FROM orders;
-- 20 of 3,020

-- the same for email, over rows where the email is not empty
SELECT count(email) - count(DISTINCT email), count(email) FROM customers;
-- 10 of 460
```

`count(*)` counts every row, while `count(DISTINCT order_id)` counts only the different `order_id` values. Subtracting one from the other gives the number of extra rows. The first table is 99.34% unique and the email table 97.83%, and both numbers match what was planted (run: `02_dimensions.py`).

The 20 is counted as "extra rows", which is the number a cleanup job would delete. We can also count another way: all the rows that belong to a duplicate group, which here is 40 rows (the 20 repeated orders each take two rows). That is the number of rows a person has to look at. Both are correct. But if one report says "20 of 3,020" and another says "40 of 3,020", readers will think the data changed. Choose one, write which one in the rule, and keep the other as a note (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`).

</Step>

## A key can be unique while the thing is not

Back to the class register. `customer_id` is unique across all 500 rows. If we wrote a rule that `customer_id` must not repeat, it would pass. But there are 460 emails that are not empty, and they hold only 450 different values (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). A rule that checks the wrong key misses this problem. Deciding which column, or which set of columns, identifies "the same customer" is a business decision, and the check has to be written to match it.

Even with the right key, text that differs only slightly can slip past the count. This table shows 4 made-up emails.

| Email as stored | After lowercasing and trimming trailing spaces |
|---|---|
| `ann@example.com` | `ann@example.com` |
| `Ann@Example.com` | `ann@example.com` |
| `ann@example.com ` (with a trailing space) | `ann@example.com` |
| `bob@example.com` | `bob@example.com` |

A plain `count(DISTINCT email)` gives 4, because each text differs when we compare character by character. After `lower(trim(email))` there are only 2 values, so there are 2 people, not 4. Making text uniform (standardizing) before comparing finds more duplicates, and Ten Steps notes that tools differ in whether they standardize before comparing (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 140 to 146).

</Step>

<Step>

## DISTINCT can hide a defect

A team that finds repeated orders often fixes them by adding `SELECT DISTINCT` to a model. The output of the model is right at once, but the source keeps sending the repeated rows.

See what it does to our revenue. The sum of `total_amount` in `orders` as it stands is 7,616,911.36, but after removing the copies that are identical in every column it is 7,560,842.09. So the repeated rows overstate revenue by 56,069.27 (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`). Anyone who reads the raw table without going through that model still gets the wrong total.

If we put a uniqueness check after the `DISTINCT`, that check will always pass, because the data it sees has already been cleaned. So we should check the table the source sends, before anything touches it.

</Step>

<Step>

## How it differs from the other dimensions

Uniqueness and integrity are so close that DMBOK2 puts repeated rows in the description of integrity as well, calling them identical rows that distort aggregation (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459). If we file a repeated `order_id` under uniqueness, we should not count it again under [Integrity](/en/data-quality/dimensions/integrity/), or the scorecard counts the same rows twice. The two differ in what they look for: integrity looks for keys that point at something that does not exist (order 65 points to customer 9017, who is not there), while uniqueness looks for keys that appear too often. In our dataset there are 30 orphan rows and 20 extra order rows.

Repeated rows can also pass validity perfectly. All 20 customers who share an email have a well formed email, as the [Validity](/en/data-quality/dimensions/validity/) page showed. And a customer recorded twice with slightly different spelling, such as `Ann@Example.com`, is a consistency problem until we decide they are the same person (see the page [Consistency](/en/data-quality/dimensions/consistency/)).

</Step>
