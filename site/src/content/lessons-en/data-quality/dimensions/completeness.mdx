## Completeness asks whether everything that should be there is there

Imagine the marketing team about to send a promotion by email to every customer. They open the `customers` table and find this.

| customer_id | email | country |
|---:|---|---|
| 2 | `user2@example.com` | TH |
| 3 | `user3@example.com` | TH |
| 14 | (empty) | TH |
| 24 | (empty) | TH |
| 30 | (empty) | TH |

Customers 14, 24 and 30 have no email to send to. Data that should be there is missing, and that is exactly what completeness measures. DMBOK2 asks the same thing directly: is all the required data present? DAMA UK defines it as a ratio, the proportion of data stored against the potential for 100% (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458, and section 1.3.3, page 457).

The whole table has 500 customers and 40 of them have an empty email, so 460 have one, which is 92%. We measure it with this query.

```sql
SELECT count(*) FILTER (WHERE email IS NULL), count(*) FROM customers;
-- 40 of 500
```

The 92% is a count at the column level. The book says completeness can be measured at three levels. At the column level we ask whether one field is filled in. At the record level we ask whether a row is filled in the way its status requires. At the dataset level we ask whether all the rows that should be in the table have arrived. All three levels have an example on this page (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458).

</Step>

## An empty cell is not always a defect

Before we call an empty cell a defect, we should ask when the field should have a value. The book sorts the answers into three groups (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458).

| Group | Meaning | If the cell is empty | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mandatory | Every row must have a value | a defect | `order_id` in `orders`. Without it we cannot tell which order the row is |
| Conditional or optional | A value is required only when a condition holds, or it may be present or not | a defect only in rows where the condition holds. If it is truly optional, not a defect | `shipped_ts` is required when the status is SHIPPED. A phone number that the customer may leave blank is optional (our dataset has no such column) |
| Inapplicable | The field has no meaning for this row, so it must be empty | normal. A value would be the mistake | `shipped_ts` on an order that is still PLACED or was CANCELLED |

An empty cell looks the same in every group and means something different in each. Empty in a mandatory column is a defect. Empty in an inapplicable column is correct. For the middle group we need the condition before we can judge.

`orders.shipped_ts` is the clearest example, because it sits in two groups at once, depending on the status of the order.

| status | rows | rows with `shipped_ts` | What is expected |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| PLACED | 756 | 0 | empty, nothing has shipped |
| SHIPPED | 1,794 | 1,769 | filled in every row |
| CANCELLED | 470 | 0 | empty |

The whole table has 1,251 rows where `shipped_ts` is empty. If we flagged every one of them as a defect, we would be wrong about 1,226, because those are orders that have not shipped or were cancelled, and an empty cell is correct for them. The real defects are the 25 orders that are SHIPPED but have no shipping time, such as order 84, which has the status SHIPPED and an empty `shipped_ts` (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`).

```sql
SELECT count(*) FILTER (WHERE status = 'SHIPPED' AND shipped_ts IS NULL),
       count(*) FILTER (WHERE status = 'SHIPPED')
FROM orders;
-- 25 of 1,794
```

Notice that this rule has the condition `status = 'SHIPPED'` written into it. A completeness score that does not say which group of rows it measures is a score nobody can interpret.

</Step>

## A cell that is filled but holds no real data

`IS NULL` finds only the cells that are truly empty. People who fill in forms often get past a required field by typing something into it. The phone table below is a small made-up table of 8 rows that we created to show this.

| phone | Does the cell hold real data? | Does `IS NULL` see it? |
|---|---|---|
| 0812345678 | yes | no (correct) |
| (empty string) | no | no |
| N/A | no | no |
| 000-000-0000 | no | no |
| (NULL) | no | yes |
| 0898765432 | yes | no (correct) |
| (two spaces) | no | no |
| - | no | no |

Six cells in this table hold no real data, and `IS NULL` sees only one of them. A check that also looks for empty strings, blanks and placeholders such as `N/A`, `-` and `000-000-0000` finds all six (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`).

Ten Steps advises us to look for default values, and for the habit of typing something into a required field just to get past it, such as a single period to finish saving the record. Some teams decide that a field holding only the default counts as not filled in. The easiest way to find these values is to look at the most frequent values in the column, as [lesson 3](/en/data-quality/profiling/#look-at-the-frequency-of-values-in-columns-with-only-a-few-values) teaches, because placeholders always appear at the top of the list (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 128 to 135).

</Step>

## Every row is complete, but the table is not

A column-level rule sees only the rows that have already arrived in the table. If a whole day of rows never loaded, the rows that did load are still fully filled in and the score is still 100%.

Our dataset has no day with missing rows, so to show the idea we made a copy of `orders` and removed 4 of every 5 orders of 30 August. That day went from 30 orders to 6. A row-level check that asks whether `status` and `total_amount` have values passes 2,976 of 2,976 rows, because every row that remains is complete.

What tells us something is wrong is a comparison with what is normal on other days. This table receives 24.8 orders a day on average, and the number moves up and down by about 4.7 orders (the standard deviation). We say a day more than three times that amount below the average is unusual, which means a day with fewer than about 10.6 orders. The day with 6 orders is far below that line (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`).

The book says a dataset-level measurement can be done in two ways. One is to compare with a source of record, such as the row count in the source system, which [lesson 8](/en/data-quality/reconciliation/#reconcile-with-row-counts-and-sums) teaches. The other is to compare with how full this table has been in the past, which the book calls its historical levels of population (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458). The second way needs no source to compare with, but it has a cost: a day that is quiet for a real business reason, such as a long holiday, looks exactly like a day with missing rows.

</Step>

## The denominator changes the score

A completeness score depends on what we divide by. `customers.email` is 92.00% complete out of 500 customers, but the rule that checks the format of emails uses 460 as its denominator, because the 40 empty emails were already counted here. We do not want one empty cell to fail two rules at once. Using a different denominator in each rule is fine, as long as each rule says what it divides by (the summary table in the lesson [Data quality dimensions](/en/data-quality/dimensions/#all-14-rules-together) shows the denominator of all 14 rules).

</Step>

## Why look at completeness first

The author of Ten Steps tells of a deduplication project they once worked on. The team picked a field they "knew" was essential for deciding whether two records belong to the same person, then tuned the algorithm over many rounds without getting good results. When they finally checked the fill rate, that field had a value in only 20% of the records. The algorithm was not at fault. The data it needed had never been there (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 42 to 46). So if we are about to build logic that depends on a field, we check the completeness of that field first.

</Step>

<Step>

## How it differs from the other dimensions

Completeness and validity are separated by the question they ask. Customer 14 has no email at all, which is completeness. Customer 23 has an email, but it is `user23example.com` without the `@`, which is validity, and the [Validity page](/en/data-quality/dimensions/validity/) explains it further. In `orders`, an empty `customer_id` (order 344, for example) is completeness, while a `customer_id` of 9017 for a customer who does not exist (order 65) is integrity, which the [Integrity page](/en/data-quality/dimensions/integrity/) covers. Together those two groups make 45 orders that cannot be tied to a customer.

A field that is filled in can still be wrong. `birth_date` is filled in for all 500 customers, but customer 28 has a birth date of 2026-12-25, a day in the future, and completeness cannot see that at all.

Ten Steps does not treat completeness and validity as separate dimensions. They sit together in data integrity fundamentals, because one round of profiling finds both. The book also says that assessing and reporting are different things, so we may still report them separately (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 42 to 46).

</Step>

## Check yourself

- Pick one column in a table you own. Is it mandatory, conditional or inapplicable, and does the rule that checks it say so?
- Your pipeline loads 2% fewer rows today than yesterday, and every loaded row is fully populated. Which level of completeness has failed, and which check would have seen it?
- Find a column where people type a placeholder to get past a required field. How would you count those placeholders?
