## What the word asks

DMBOK2 defines accuracy as the degree to which data correctly represents real-life entities. It adds that accuracy is difficult to measure unless you can reproduce the data collection or confirm records by hand, and that most measures compare the data with a source that has been verified as accurate, such as a system of record or reliable external reference data (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 458). DAMA UK says the same in other words: the degree to which data correctly describes the real world object or event (book: DMBOK2 section 1.3.3, page 457).

Picture a thermometer. A reading of 31.4 has the right format and a plausible range. Whether the room is 31.4 degrees takes a second thermometer that you trust, or a person who knows.

Ten Steps opens its accuracy step with the observation that many people use "accuracy" as a synonym for data quality as a whole, and that data professionals know the two are not the same (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 135 to 140).

</Step>

## What profiling can and cannot tell you

Ten Steps gives three examples of the line between what profiling shows and what only a comparison can show (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 135 to 140):

| Profiling shows | Only a comparison shows |
|---|---|
| Every part has a valid code, M for make or B for buy | Whether each code is the way that part is actually sourced |
| Postal codes sit in the postal code field in valid patterns | Whether it is the right postal code for this customer |
| The stock count has the right data type | Whether the number matches what is on the shelf |

In our dataset the comparison is between two systems. The payment system is treated as the source of truth, and each non-cancelled order total is compared with the sum of its payments:

```sql
WITH o AS (SELECT DISTINCT order_id, total_amount FROM orders WHERE status <> 'CANCELLED'),
     p AS (SELECT order_id, sum(amount) AS paid FROM payments GROUP BY order_id)
SELECT count(*) FILTER (WHERE abs(o.total_amount - p.paid) > 0.005), count(*)
FROM o JOIN p USING (order_id);
-- 35 of 2,533, so 98.62% accurate against payments
```

| order_id | total_amount | paid | difference |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 91 | 487.71 | 437.71 | -50.00 |
| 122 | 2029.28 | 1979.28 | -50.00 |
| 412 | 4147.84 | 4146.84 | -1.00 |
| 438 | 1716.14 | 1836.14 | 120.00 |

## Meanings that get misread

<Callout type="warn" title="1. You cannot read accuracy off a column">
Without a reference you can check validity, and you cannot check accuracy. The Ten Steps decision flow has four questions: can you identify an authoritative source, can you get access to it, do the stakeholders agree that it is the authority, and can you afford the assessment. If any answer is no, the book says you cannot or should not assess accuracy, and should use other dimensions instead (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 135 to 140). "We are 98.62% accurate" is a sentence with a hidden clause: "against the payment system".
</Callout>

**2. A mismatch says two sources disagree, not which one is wrong.** Our 35 rows could be an order total that was typed wrong, or a payment that was captured wrong. We call them inaccurate orders because we agreed that payments are the authority. 20 payments name an order that does not exist (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`), which shows that the reference has problems of its own. The book stresses that without agreement on the authoritative source you risk distrust in the results.

**3. The tolerance sets the number.** The check above counts a difference larger than half a cent. Change the tolerance and the result moves (run: `13_dimension_pitfalls.py`):

| Tolerance | Mismatches of 2,533 |
|---:|---:|
| 0.005 | 35 |
| 1.00 | 27 |
| 11.00 | 18 |
| 60.00 | 11 |

None of these is the right answer. A difference of 1.00 and a difference of 120.00 are different problems, and the people who own the money decide where the line goes. Write the tolerance into the rule.

**4. Valid is not accurate.** All 35 wrong totals are non-negative, so they pass the validity rule. DMBOK2 uses a date of birth as its example: a value inside the allowed range can still be wrong for the person it describes (book: DMBOK2 Table 29, page 459).

**5. Accuracy is expensive, so measure a part.** The book says that accuracy checks are often manual and costly, and suggests asking whether a smaller number of records or fewer fields would still give meaningful results (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 135 to 140). In our data the comparison is a join and costs nothing, which is the exception.

**6. The word is also a model metric.** In machine learning "accuracy" is the share of correct predictions. It does not mean that the training data is accurate in the sense used here, so say which one you mean (this remark is the author's).

</Step>

## Easy to confuse with

| Looks like | How to tell them apart | In our data |
|---|---|---|
| Validity | Validity asks if the value is allowed. Accuracy asks if it is true | 35 of 35 wrong totals are valid |
| Consistency | Agreement between two sources does not prove either right | a copy always agrees with its source |
| Reconciliation | Reconciliation is the process of comparing two systems. Accuracy is what a good match tells you about one of them ([lesson 8](/en/data-quality/reconciliation/#reconcile-with-row-counts-and-sums)) | order totals against payment totals |

</Step>
