## From parts to a project

[Lessons 1](/en/data-quality/assurance-basics/) to [10](/en/data-quality/ownership-scorecard/) gave you the parts: dimensions, profiling, rules, checkpoints, contracts, monitoring, reconciliation, incident handling and a scorecard. A real piece of work starts earlier than any of them. Someone says the marketing emails keep bouncing, and you have to decide what to look at, in what order, for whom, and how to get anyone to act on the result.

*Executing Data Quality Projects* by Danette McGilvray is a method for exactly that. It is called the Ten Steps. It is written for projects, not for a permanent system, and it says you do not have to run every step: you can pick the ones that serve the business need, go back to an earlier step when you learn something, and repeat the whole sequence later (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 66 to 68).

</Step>

## Start from the business need

Step 1 is about choosing the business need and the issues to work on, then planning the project. The book defines business needs as whatever matters to the organization, driven by customers, products, services, strategies, goals, issues and opportunities, and says the most critical ones should drive every decision in the project (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 78 to 91).

That is [lesson 1](/en/data-quality/assurance-basics/#quality-means-fit-for-use) again, with a project attached. "Customers should have a usable email" is only a data quality problem because marketing has a campaign that needs it. If you cannot name the need, you cannot choose a threshold or defend the effort. The book says that doing Step 1 badly guarantees failure, partial success, or a lot of work on the wrong thing, and that many projects have failed because of misunderstanding between the people involved (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, page 90).

Each step ends with a checkpoint of questions to answer before moving on. Step 1's include: are the needs, issues, focus, benefits and objectives clearly defined; have the sponsors and stakeholders been engaged and do they support it; have the resources been committed (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 78 to 91).

</Step>

## Follow the data through its life

Step 2 asks you to understand the environment that produced the problem. One tool the book gives for that is the information life cycle, which it calls POSMAD after its six phases (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 37 to 41):

| Phase | Meaning | Example activities |
|---|---|---|
| Plan | Prepare for the data | Set objectives, design the information architecture, write standards and definitions |
| Obtain | Acquire it | Create records, buy data, load external files |
| Store and Share | Hold it and make it reachable | Store in a database, expose through screens, reports and APIs |
| Maintain | Keep it working | Update, validate, standardize, deduplicate, merge, cleanse |
| Apply | Use it | Complete a transaction, write a report, make a decision, run an automated process |
| Dispose | Remove it when no longer useful | Archive, delete |

The book's point is that quality is affected by activities in every phase, but a project has to pick the phases it will address. Its own example is a team that had spent its effort on Obtain and nothing on Maintain, so its next project went to Maintain. For any problem it suggests asking which phase the problem shows up in, what happened to the data in earlier phases, and how later phases will be affected (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 37 to 41).

On our data, placing each defect in a phase is a useful first pass (author's reading): blank emails and the five spellings of Thailand enter at Obtain, duplicate order ids come from Obtain or Maintain depending on whether the source or the load repeated them, and the payments that point at no order are a Maintain problem between two systems. The book also notes that "lineage" in vendor tools is another name for the same idea.

</Step>

## Choose what to assess, and what to skip

The book's dimension list is organized by how you would assess each dimension, not by the usual DAMA names. Its substeps are: perception of relevance and trust, data specifications, data integrity fundamentals, accuracy, uniqueness and deduplication, consistency and synchronization, timeliness, access, security and privacy, presentation quality, data coverage, data decay, and usability and transactability (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 42 to 46; chapter 4, pages 119 to 167). Completeness and validity are not separate there. They are parts of data integrity fundamentals, which uses profiling to look at fill rate, validity, structure and content.

Its advice on picking is short:

- Start from the business need, then list candidate dimensions, then finalize the list at the start of Step 3.
- Ask two questions of each dimension. *Should I assess it?* Only if the result will give you something you can act on. *Can I assess it?* Only if it is possible and affordable. Skip the dimension unless both answers are yes.
- If you still have too many, rank them with a benefit and cost matrix (described under business impact below).
- Do data integrity fundamentals first, even if your worry is something else (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 42 to 46).

The last point comes with a story. A team worried about duplicates went straight to deduplication, chose the fields that should identify a record, and tuned the matching again and again without good results. When they finally profiled the data, one of the fields they had counted on to identify records was only 20% filled in. The book's moral is to look at the data you depend on before building anything on it (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 42 to 46). That is [lesson 3](/en/data-quality/profiling/#why-look-before-writing-rules): profile first.

</Step>

## Show what it is worth

When a data quality problem is found, the first thing management says is "so what?", according to the book. Step 4 exists to answer that, and the book lists eleven techniques, ordered roughly from least to most effort (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 47 to 49; chapter 4, pages 168 to 205):

| Technique | What it is |
|---|---|
| Anecdotes | Examples of the effect of poor data, told as a story for the audience. The cheapest technique |
| Connect the dots | Show the link from a business need to the data that supports it |
| Usage | List the processes and people that use the data |
| Five whys for business impact | Ask why, who, what, where and when repeatedly to reach the real effect |
| Process impact | Show the workarounds, rework and wasted time the data causes |
| Risk analysis | List what could go wrong, how likely and how severe, and how to reduce it |
| Perception of relevance and trust | Survey the people who use and maintain the data |
| Benefit vs. cost matrix | Rate issues, dimensions or recommendations on benefit and on cost |
| Ranking and prioritization | Rank the effect of missing or wrong data on specific processes |
| Cost of low-quality data | Put a money figure on waste, rework, missed revenue and lost business |
| Cost-benefit analysis and ROI | Compare what an investment costs with what it returns |

Two cautions from the book. Showing impact does not always mean a full cost analysis, because a technique that takes less effort is not less useful, and every project can and should assess something. And who needs convincing decides which technique to use: an anecdote may be enough to recruit a subject matter expert, and a finance approval may need the full cost-benefit analysis (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 47 to 49).

The benefit vs. cost matrix is the one technique you can use at several points, not only in Step 4. It sorts a list into four quadrants (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 191 to 194):

| | Low cost | High cost |
|---|---|---|
| High benefit | Quick wins: the issues most likely to be addressed first | Gems: very valuable but need much time, effort and resources. May go before quick wins if the benefit outweighs the cost |
| Low benefit | Connectors: unlikely, unless they can be done together with a quick win or a gem | Avoid: burns time and money for little gain |

The book lists what you can prioritize with it: the issues to build the project around (Step 1), the dimensions to assess (Step 3), the issues worth a root cause analysis (Step 5) and the recommendations that get a detailed plan (Step 6).

</Step>

## Find the cause, then improve in three directions

Step 5 uses three techniques: five whys for root causes, track and trace (follow the data through its life cycle, compare it where it enters and leaves each stage, and find where the problem first appears) and the cause-and-effect or fishbone diagram. The book gives common categories to hang causes on: the 4 M's (machines, methods, materials, manpower), the 4 P's (policies, procedures, people, plant), plus environment, measurement and management (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 206 to 219). [Lesson 9](/en/data-quality/incidents/#root-cause-analysis-dqfs-five-steps) walked the five whys and a track-and-trace on the duplicated orders.

Two points from the book's notes on this step. Use a neutral facilitator who is not invested in the outcome, so that everyone gets heard. And keep the session safe: people need to feel they can talk freely without repercussions (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 206 to 219). That is the blameless tone of [lesson 9](/en/data-quality/incidents/#a-blameless-postmortem)'s postmortem, applied to the analysis itself.

Steps 6 to 9 form an improvement cycle (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, pages 66 to 68). The book asks that every recommendation be tagged as preventing, correcting or detecting, so that each of the three has been considered. The first idea is nearly always to correct the data, and the book says to add prevention and detection beside it (book: Ten Steps chapter 4, pages 220 to 241):

| Direction | Step | What it does | This path |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prevent | 7 | Stop the error from entering again | Checks at the source, contracts ([lessons 5](/en/data-quality/checkpoints/#prevent-it-at-the-source) and [6](/en/data-quality/agreements/#why-you-need-a-contract)) |
| Correct | 8 | Fix the data that is already wrong | Quarantine, backfill ([lessons 5](/en/data-quality/checkpoints/#when-a-check-fails-let-it-through-quarantine-or-stop) and [9](/en/data-quality/incidents/#fix-temporary-first-then-permanent)) |
| Detect | 9 | Monitor controls and the data | Monitoring, reconciliation ([lessons 7](/en/data-quality/monitoring/) and [8](/en/data-quality/reconciliation/)) |

Step 6 also requires an owner for every improvement, someone who has accepted the accountability.

</Step>

<Step>

## Bring people along

Step 10 is the bar under the others. The book's suggestions repeat from step to step: keep a stakeholder list and a communication plan, listen to feedback including the negative, hold a kickoff, keep status reports flowing, track issues and actions in one place, and get agreement before moving on. It says plainly that communication runs both ways and that you open a dialogue and gauge reaction (book: Ten Steps chapter 3, page 66; chapter 4, pages 242 to 252).

This part is easy to skip in technical work, and the book treats it as part of every step. [Lesson 10](/en/data-quality/ownership-scorecard/#who-is-responsible) covered who is responsible for data and how to report. This is the same subject seen from the person running the project.

</Step>

## Apply it to the customers email problem

The earlier lessons already fill in the steps for one project. This table uses only what you have seen, and nothing in the last column is new data.

| Step | For "customers need a usable email" | From |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Need | Marketing sends campaigns by email. Target of 98% usable, the DMBOK2 example from [lesson 1](/en/data-quality/assurance-basics/#quality-means-fit-for-use) | [Lesson 1](/en/data-quality/assurance-basics/#quality-means-fit-for-use) |
| 2. Environment | Placed at Obtain: the sign-up form and the source system. Customers table owned by crm-team | [Lessons 3](/en/data-quality/profiling/) and [10](/en/data-quality/ownership-scorecard/#who-is-responsible) |
| 3. Assess | Data integrity fundamentals first: 40 blank, 15 without an `@`, 10 duplicated. Then validity and uniqueness rules | [Lessons 2](/en/data-quality/dimensions/) to [4](/en/data-quality/rule-design/) |
| 4. Impact | 445 of 500 usable (89.0%) against a line of 98%. An anecdote from marketing would sit beside the number | [Lesson 1](/en/data-quality/assurance-basics/#the-price-of-bad-data) |
| 5. Root cause | Ask how the form lets an email be blank or malformed | [Lesson 9](/en/data-quality/incidents/#root-cause-analysis-dqfs-five-steps) |
| 6 to 9. Improve | Prevent: a required, validated field on the form. Correct: fix or quarantine the 55 bad rows. Detect: a monitored rule with an owner | [Lessons 4](/en/data-quality/rule-design/#the-rule-registry-a-query-with-an-owner-a-threshold-and-an-impact), [5](/en/data-quality/checkpoints/), [7](/en/data-quality/monitoring/) |
| 10. People | Tell marketing and crm-team what is changing and when, and get the form change on their backlog | [Lesson 10](/en/data-quality/ownership-scorecard/#who-is-responsible) |

(The numbers are the ones from [lessons 1](/en/data-quality/assurance-basics/) to [3](/en/data-quality/profiling/). The way they fill the steps is the author's.)
